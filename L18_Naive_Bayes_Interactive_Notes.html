Introduction

Spam or Ham?

Imagine you're a Data Scientist at Google, building the spam filter for Gmail. Every day, millions of emails arrive, and each one needs a decision: is this spam or ham (not spam)?

Mail 1

"Congratulations! You have WON a FREE lottery prize, click now!!!"

Mail 2

"Please review the project report before our meeting."

Obviously, Mail 1 is spam. Ask yourself: how did you know that, instantly, without thinking hard? Certain words — "FREE," "WON," "click now" — are strong spam signals. A classifier that simply learns which words tend to appear in spam vs ham should already do a reasonable job.
Today's algorithm builds exactly that idea into real math — and it's called Naive Bayes. The name is a warning label built right in: it makes one deliberately naive (oversimplified) assumption to make the math tractable. We'll see exactly what that assumption is, and why it works anyway.

What Will Be the Input?

Ask yourself: to classify an email as spam or ham, what does the model actually receive as input?

The input is text — the raw body of the email. Not a number, not a category from a fixed list. Just a sequence of words the sender typed.

Every model we've studied so far — Decision Trees, Random Forest, KNN, Logistic Regression — expects numbers as input. Text is not numbers. So the very first question has to be: what do we do if our data is text?
Cleaning Text

Step 1 — Tokenization

Before a model can count words, it needs to know where one word ends and the next begins. Take: "To be or not to be."

"To be or not to be."  →  ["To", "be", "or", "not", "to", "be", "."]
This is tokenization — breaking raw text into individual units (tokens), usually words and punctuation marks. It's the mandatory first step; nothing downstream works without it.
Ask yourself: in the tokens above, is the period "." useful for deciding spam vs ham? What about "!!!" or ","?
Generally, no — a period or comma carries almost no spam/ham signal by itself. (Some advanced setups do keep punctuation like "!!!" as a weak signal of urgency/spam — but for our purposes, we'll treat plain punctuation as noise and remove it, exactly like the stopword step coming up.)

Step 2 — Upper Case or Lower Case?

Compare: "FREE money" vs "free money."

Ask yourself: should the model treat "FREE" and "free" as two different words, or the same word?
If we don't normalize case, "FREE" and "free" become two separate vocabulary entries — splitting one strong spam signal into two weaker ones, and undercounting how often that word really appears.
Fix: lowercase everything. "FREE" → "free". Now every occurrence of that word — however it was typed — gets counted as the same word.
Careful: for THIS course, we lowercase everything. But note there ARE settings where case carries real signal (e.g. "US" the country vs "us" the pronoun) — that's a deeper NLP nuance for a dedicated text-processing module, not needed for the Naive Bayes math today.

Step 3 — Stopword Removal

Take: "This is a river."

Ask yourself: does the word "is" help decide anything about spam vs ham? Does "a"? Does "the"?
No — words like "a," "the," "is," "to," "for" appear constantly in every kind of text, spam or ham. They carry almost no information about the class.
These extremely common, low-information words are called stopwords, and we remove them. What's left behind is a shorter list of words that actually carry signal.
tokenize → lowercase → remove punctuation → remove stopwords  =  cleaned email

The Goal

What Are We Actually Predicting?

Define spam=1, ham=0. Given a cleaned email, we want to compare exactly two numbers:

P(y=1 | email)   vs   P(y=0 | email)

Whichever is bigger becomes our prediction. That's the whole decision rule. The only open question: how do we actually calculate P(y=1 | email)?

This is a conditional probability, and we don't yet have a direct way to compute it from training data. There's a classical tool built exactly for reversing conditional probabilities: Bayes' theorem. Let's build it from scratch.
Bayes Theorem

India vs Virat — A Table to Build Intuition

Forget email for a moment. Two events, from 100 historical India matches:

Every probability below is a ratio read directly off this table — nothing assumed, everything counted.

QuantityRead asValue

Deriving Bayes Theorem

Ask yourself: given that India won (A), what's the chance Virat scored a century that match — P(B|A)? Don't read it off the table yet; derive it.
STEP 1 Get the joint probability first: P(A∩B) = P(A|B)·P(B) = (10/12)×(12/100) = 10/100 = 0.10.
STEP 2 Reverse it: P(B|A) = P(A∩B)/P(A) = 0.10/0.60 = 1/6 ≈ 0.167.
STEP 3 Check against the table directly: cell(win,century)/row-total(win) = 10/60 = 0.167 ✓ — matches exactly.

Generalize this exact swap. We had two ways to write the same joint probability:

P(A∩B) = P(A|B)·P(B)   =   P(B|A)·P(A)

Set the right-hand sides equal to each other and rearrange for P(A|B):

P(A|B) = P(B|A) · P(A) / P(B)  ← Bayes' Theorem

What Bayes Theorem Actually Means

TermNameIn sentence form
P(A|B)Posterior"What I believe about A, after seeing evidence B."
P(B|A)Likelihood"If A were true, how compatible is evidence B with that?"
P(A)Prior"What I believed about A before seeing any evidence."
P(B)Evidence"How common is B overall, across every possibility?"
One sentence to hold onto: Bayes' theorem turns a prior belief into a posterior belief, using how well new evidence fits each hypothesis, adjusted for how common that evidence is in general.
Cricket example, in words: "Before knowing anything else, India wins 60% of matches (prior). Virat scoring a century is fairly rare evidence (12%). But when that evidence DOES show up, India's win rate jumps to 83.3% (posterior) — a century is strong evidence in India's favour."
Applying Bayes to Email

P(y=1|email) and P(y=0|email)

Apply Bayes' theorem directly, treating the class as our "A" and the email text as our "B":

P(y=1|email) = P(email|y=1)·P(y=1) / P(email)
P(y=0|email) = P(email|y=0)·P(y=0) / P(email)
QuantityMeaningEasy or hard to get?
P(y=1)class prioreasy — just count
P(email|y=1)likelihoodeasy — count word occurrences in spam
P(y=1|email)posterior — what we wanthard to get directly

Why We Can Drop the Denominator

Both expressions divide by the exact same thing: P(email). We only care which of the two is bigger — and dividing two numbers by the same positive constant never changes which one is bigger.

Example: is 6 > 4? Yes. Divide both by 2: is 3 > 2? Still yes. The comparison survives — only the actual magnitude of both numbers shrank equally.
P(y=1|email) ∝ P(email|y=1)·P(y=1)     P(y=0|email) ∝ P(email|y=0)·P(y=0)
We only need the right-hand sides now — no need to ever compute P(email) at all. This single simplification is what makes Naive Bayes fast.

Finding P(y=1) and P(y=0)

Straight from our 10-email dataset: 5 spam, 5 ham.

P(y=1) = 5/10 = 0.5     P(y=0) = 5/10 = 0.5
These are called the class priors — the probability of each class before looking at any specific email's words. Trivially easy: count training rows per class, divide by total.

What Is "Text", and How Do We Store It?

Ask yourself: P(email|y=1) means "given spam, what's the probability of seeing this EXACT sequence of words?" Why is that a hard number to estimate directly?

A cleaned email is a sequence of words: text = w1, w2, ..., wd. To estimate P(text|y=1) directly, we'd need training spam emails containing this exact word combination — with even a modest vocabulary, almost no two emails share one. That probability collapses toward zero from sparsity alone.

We'll fix this shortly with the naive assumption. But first: how do we even store a whole vocabulary of words efficiently?
Answer: a count vectorizer. Build one shared vocabulary across all training emails, then represent each email as a row of word counts — one column per vocabulary word, the count of how many times it appears in that email. Most entries are 0 (any single email only uses a handful of the whole vocabulary) — a sparse matrix.
The Naive Assumption

Conditional Independence, With Easy Examples

Instead of needing the exact word combination, assume each word's occurrence is independent of every other word's, given the class:

P(w1,w2,...,wd | y) ≈ P(w1|y)·P(w2|y)·...·P(wd|y)
Now we only ever need to count how often each individual word appears in spam vs ham — a far easier, far more data-efficient number to estimate than "how often does this whole combination appear."

Two easy examples, outside email entirely

Weather example

"Given that it's summer (the class), is it raining independent of whether it's windy?" Not perfectly true in reality (storms bring both) — but assuming so lets us estimate P(rain|summer) and P(wind|summer) separately, instead of needing exact joint weather records for "rain AND wind together, in summer."

Grocery example

"Given that this shopper is buying for a party (the class), is buying chips independent of buying soda?" Not really — they often go together. But assuming independence still lets a store predict "party shopper" from chips and soda separately, without needing every historical combination.

Why the assumption is "naive": words like "Nigerian" and "prince" are NOT actually independent — they tend to appear together in scam emails. The assumption is a deliberate simplification, not a claim that it's literally true. It's naive on purpose, to make the math tractable — and it works well enough in practice anyway.
Common interview trip-up: the independence is conditioned on the class — it's the CONDITIONAL probability P(w1,w2|y) that factorizes, not the marginal (unconditional) probability. Two words can be globally correlated across all emails, yet still treated as independent once you already know the class.

Computing P(text|y=1) and P(text|y=0)

For a single word wj: go through all spam training emails, count total spam word-occurrences (n1), and how many of those occurrences were wj (nj1):

P(wj|y=1) = nj1 / n1

This raw counting approach (does the word exist / how many times) is called Bernoulli / Multinomial Naive Bayes depending on whether we count presence or frequency — more on this distinction in §Bernoulli-Multinomial below.

Ask yourself: where was the loss function? Where were the gradient updates, the iterations?
There weren't any. Naive Bayes' "training" is not iterative optimization at all — it's a single pass of counting.

That's it. Training = building these count tables once. This is exactly why Naive Bayes is famous for being extremely fast — even over millions of emails, it's one linear pass through the data.

The Zero Problem

Try a slightly different new email:

"Free cash bonus now" → cleaned: [free, cash, bonus, now]
"cash" and "bonus" never appeared anywhere in training — not in spam, not in ham. They're not in our vocabulary at all.
P(cash|spam) = 0/37 = 0    P(cash|ham) = 0/28 = 0
score(spam) = P(y=1) × P(free|1) × P(cash|1) × P(bonus|1) × P(now|1) = ... × 0 × ... = 0
score(ham) = P(y=0) × P(free|0) × P(cash|0) × P(bonus|0) × P(now|0) = ... × 0 × ... = 0
This is the Zero Problem. One single missing word forces the ENTIRE product to zero — for BOTH classes — even though "free" and "now" are still overwhelming spam signals on their own. A single multiplication by zero destroys every other word's evidence, no matter how strong.
Laplace Smoothing

The Formula, and Why It Makes Sense

Fix: never let a count be exactly 0. Add a small constant α to every numerator:

P(wj|y) = (nj + α) / (n + α·V)
SymbolMeaning
njraw count of word wj in this class
ntotal word occurrences in this class
Vvocabulary size (all unique words seen anywhere)
αsmoothing strength — a hyperparameter
Ask yourself: why does the denominator need "+α·V" — why not just "+α"?
Smoothing conceptually adds α "pretend occurrences" of every single word in the vocabulary — not just the missing one. That's V separate top-ups, total added mass = α·V. For probabilities to still sum to 1 across the whole vocabulary, the denominator must absorb that same total — hence +α·V, not just +α.
Check it With V=3 words having counts 5,3,2 (n=10), α=1: probabilities become 6/13, 4/13, 3/13 → sum = 13/13 = 1.0 ✓. Using just "+α" (denominator=11) instead gives 6/11+4/11+3/11 = 1.18 — not a valid probability distribution.

Bias-Variance via Alpha

α too large → underfitting

If α >> n, the constant dominates every likelihood, pushing them all toward the same flat value regardless of real word counts. The model loses its ability to tell words apart — high bias.

α too small → overfitting

As α→0, we approach the raw, unsmoothed frequency again — any unseen word combination collapses back toward the zero problem — high variance.

α=1 (Laplace's original choice) is the standard default — a small, safe nudge, not an override. It's tuned via cross-validation like any other hyperparameter.

Recomputing Our Prediction, Smoothed

Same email — "free cash bonus now" — now with α=1, V=49, nspam=37, nham=28:

WordP(word|spam)P(word|ham)
free(5+1)/(37+49) = 0.0698(0+1)/(28+49) = 0.0130
cash(0+1)/(37+49) = 0.0116(0+1)/(28+49) = 0.0130
bonus(0+1)/(37+49) = 0.0116(0+1)/(28+49) = 0.0130
now(4+1)/(37+49) = 0.0581(0+1)/(28+49) = 0.0130
score(spam) 0.5 × 0.0698 × 0.0116 × 0.0116 × 0.0581 = 2.74 × 10-7
score(ham) 0.5 × 0.0130 × 0.0130 × 0.0130 × 0.0130 = 1.42 × 10-8
Both scores are non-zero now — smoothing did exactly its job. Neither collapsed to zero, so the model can actually compare them.

Sum ≠ 1 — Normalizing to Get the Answer

Ask yourself: do score(spam) and score(ham) add up to 1? Should they?
2.74×10-7 + 1.42×10-8 = 2.88×10-7  ← NOT 1
No, they don't sum to 1 — and they were never supposed to. Remember §drop-denom: we deliberately dropped P(email), the normalizing denominator, because we only needed to compare the two scores. What's left are un-normalized proportional scores, not true probabilities.
Fix: normalize. Divide each score by their sum, so they properly add up to 1:
P(spam|X) = score(spam) / [score(spam)+score(ham)] = 2.74e-7 / 2.88e-7 = 0.951
P(ham|X) = score(ham) / [score(spam)+score(ham)] = 1.42e-8 / 2.88e-7 = 0.049
0.951 + 0.049 = 1.0 ✓ — now we have real probabilities. Since 0.951 > 0.049, the model predicts spam — correctly, despite two words ("cash," "bonus") never having been seen in training at all.
Is This Regression?

Why Naive Bayes Is Not Regression

Ask yourself: could we use this exact same machinery to predict a continuous number — like a house price?
No. Every step we built — priors P(y), likelihoods P(word|y), Bayes' theorem, normalization — is built around comparing a small, fixed set of discrete classes (spam vs ham; or class A vs B vs C). There's no continuous target anywhere in this machinery.
Naive Bayes is a probabilistic classifier. Its output is always "which class is most likely," expressed as a probability distribution over a fixed set of classes — never a continuous number like "₹52,00,000."
Other Feature Types

Categorical Features

Ask yourself: everything so far has been text. What if a feature is categorical instead — like "browser type"?

Same exact skeleton — prior × likelihood, per feature — just count categories instead of words. Example: predict bot vs human from browser + device.

#BrowserDeviceClass
1ChromeMobilehuman
2ChromeMobilehuman
3SafariDesktophuman
4EdgeDesktopbot
5ChromeDesktopbot
6SafariMobilebot
P(category|class) = (count(category,class) + α) / (nclass + K·α)

K = number of distinct values THIS feature can take (browser: K=3; device: K=2) — each feature smoothed against its own category count, not a shared vocabulary.

Test: Chrome + Desktop P(Chrome|bot)=(1+1)/(3+3)=0.333, P(Desktop|bot)=(2+1)/(3+2)=0.6 → score(bot)=0.5×0.333×0.6=0.100
Test: Chrome + Desktop P(Chrome|human)=(2+1)/(3+3)=0.5, P(Desktop|human)=(1+1)/(3+2)=0.4 → score(human)=0.5×0.5×0.4=0.100
A genuine, mathematically correct tie — Chrome leans human, Desktop leans bot, and the two effects cancel exactly. This is a real strength of the probabilistic framing: it doesn't force confidence when evidence is genuinely balanced.

Bernoulli vs Multinomial NB

Two ways to represent a word in an email — presence, or count. The choice changes the likelihood formula.

Email: "free free money"
Representation"free" valueWhat's stored
Bernoulli1did "free" appear at all? (yes/no only — repeats ignored)
Multinomial2how many TIMES did "free" appear?

Bernoulli NB

P(wj|y) is estimated as "fraction of class-y documents that contain wj at all" — repeats in one email don't count extra.

Multinomial NB

P(wj|y) is estimated as "fraction of class-y word-occurrences that were wj" — a word appearing 5 times contributes 5× the count. This is what we used throughout today's class.

Multinomial captures more information (frequency matters), at slightly more computation. Bernoulli is simpler and can work well for very short texts where repetition rarely happens anyway.

Numerical Features → Gaussian NB

Ask yourself: what if a feature is a plain number — like "account age in days" or "hours studied"? Counting doesn't make sense for continuous values.

Instead of counting, assume each numerical feature follows a Gaussian (Normal) distribution within each class — a bell curve, described by just two numbers: mean (μ) and variance (σ²).

Xj | class ~ Normal(μ, σ²)
This is genuinely the "default" Naive Bayes in most people's minds outside of text — GaussianNB in scikit-learn.
Optional deep dive — full derivation and test-point computation

Training: just compute μ and σ², per class

Feature: "hours studied." Passed: [6, 7, 8]. Failed: [1, 2, 3].

μ μpass = (6+7+8)/3 = 7.0    μfail = (1+2+3)/3 = 2.0
σ² σ²pass = [(6-7)²+(7-7)²+(8-7)²]/3 = 0.667    σ²fail = 0.667 (same computation)
That's the ENTIRE training step — two numbers per class. Compare to text, which needed a whole word-count table; here we only ever need (μ,σ²), no matter how many training rows there were.

Test time: a new student, 6.5 hours studied

p(x|class) = 1⁄√(2πσ²) × exp( −(x−μ)² ⁄ (2σ²) )
STEP 1 Plug x=6.5 into the "pass" curve (μ=7.0, σ²=0.667): p(6.5|pass) = 0.4051
STEP 2 Plug x=6.5 into the "fail" curve (μ=2.0, σ²=0.667): p(6.5|fail) = 0.000000124 (6.5 is 5.5 standard deviations from μ=2 — essentially impossible under that class)
STEP 3 Multiply by priors (0.5 each) and normalize: P(pass|x=6.5) ≈ 0.9999997, P(fail|x=6.5) ≈ 0.0000003
Notice: 6.5 was NEVER seen in training, yet the Gaussian curve still produces a fully sensible, non-zero-but-tiny probability for the unlikely class — the continuous-feature equivalent of what Laplace smoothing did for word counts.

Multiple numerical features — the naive assumption again

Add "attendance %." Evaluate each feature's density on its OWN curve, independently, then multiply — exactly the same naive assumption as multiplying word probabilities:

joint(class) = density(hours|class) × density(attendance|class)
Every numerical feature gets its own (μ,σ²) per class, evaluated completely independently of every other feature — that IS the naive assumption, applied to continuous numbers instead of discrete words.
