01 · the bridge

We already know GBDT and XGBoost. So why LightGBM?

Decision Trees ↓ Bagging / Random Forest ↓ GBDT  (first-order, sequential correction) ↓ XGBoost  (second-order + explicit regularisation) ↓ LightGBM  (same math, engineered for genuine scale)
Ask yourself: if XGBoost already uses histograms, second-order optimisation, and regularisation — what's actually left to speed up?

The computational problem, precisely

Imagine: millions of rows, thousands of features, many of them sparse, some high-cardinality categorical columns. Even with histograms, XGBoost's exact/histogram split search still has to:

  • Scan (almost) every row to build every feature's histogram, every tree, every round.
  • Grow trees level-wise — expanding every node at a depth before going deeper, even nodes with barely any gain to offer.
  • Compute split statistics on every sparse feature independently, even when most columns are mostly zero.
  • Handle categorical variables through generic numeric encodings, since it has no dedicated categorical split search.
LightGBM keeps every piece of math you already know — gradients, Hessians, Taylor approximation, regularised leaf weight, regularised Gain — and attacks exactly these four bottlenecks with four targeted engineering ideas.

02 · the bridge

Does LightGBM throw away everything we learned in XGBoost?

Ask yourself: is this a new algorithm family, or the same one, re-engineered?

No, it doesn't throw anything away. The model is still additive:

\[ F_t(x)=F_{t-1}(x)+\eta\, f_t(x) \]

Gradients and Hessians remain exactly as defined for any differentiable loss:

\[ g_i=\frac{\partial l(y_i,F_i)}{\partial F_i},\qquad h_i=\frac{\partial^2 l(y_i,F_i)}{\partial F_i^2} \]

The regularised leaf weight and split Gain are the same formulas you already derived for XGBoost:

\[ w_j^*=-\frac{G_j}{H_j+\lambda},\qquad Gain=\frac12\left(\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda}+\frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda}-\frac{G^2}{H+\lambda}\right)-\gamma \]
Throughout this chapter: SAME marks anything you already know from XGBoost — we won't re-derive it. NEW marks a genuine LightGBM idea, and those get full derivations, worked numbers, and iterations.
03 · histogram learning NEW EMPHASIS

Histogram-based learning, from first principles

A feature can easily have 100,000+ unique values in a large dataset:

1 1.03 1.07 1.12 1.18 ... 100,000 unique values
Ask yourself: do we really want to examine every possible threshold, for every feature, at every node, in every tree?

No — that's \(O(n)\) candidates per feature per split, repeated across the whole ensemble. LightGBM's answer, like XGBoost's histogram mode, is to discretize first:

Raw values: 1.1 1.2 1.3 1.8 2.1 2.3 2.7 ... ↓ bin into a histogram Bin 1: 1.0–1.5 Bin 2: 1.5–2.0 Bin 3: 2.0–2.5 Bin 4: 2.5–3.0 ...

For every bin, aggregate the gradient and Hessian of every row that falls in it:

\[ G_{\text{bin}}=\sum_{i\in\text{bin}}g_i,\qquad H_{\text{bin}}=\sum_{i\in\text{bin}}h_i \]

A candidate split after bin \(k\) then uses a cumulative (prefix) sum over bins, not over raw rows — the full step-by-step mechanics of that are in §4 below.

So what does LightGBM actually upgrade here, versus XGBoost?

Binning itself is not the upgrade — XGBoost already bins. The genuine LightGBM-emphasised upgrade is in how cheaply the histogram for each new node is built, using two compounding tricks:
#TrickWhat it buys
1Histogram subtractionA sibling's histogram is computed by pure subtraction from its parent's — zero extra row-scanning
2GOSS-reduced row scanningThe histogram that does get built from scratch is built from the smaller GOSS-sampled row set (§10–16), not the full data

Trick 2 you already understand fully from the GOSS chapter. Trick 1 is new — walked through with real numbers in §4.

Walk through the bins left to right, one at a time, keeping a running total. This running total IS \(G_L,H_L\) — there is no other computation happening:

After scanning…This bin's (G,H)Running total GLRunning total HL→ GR=G−GL→ HR=H−HLGain at this boundary

This is the entire mechanism, made explicit: at each boundary, \(G_L\) is just "add this bin's \(G\) to the running total so far." \(G_R\) is never separately scanned — it's always grand total minus the running total. We only ever touch each bin once, moving strictly left to right. The winning boundary is after Bin 1+2, Gain=11.57.

Now the LightGBM upgrade, with real numbers: histogram subtraction between siblings

The split above creates two children: Left = Bins 1+2 (ages 12–29), Right = Bins 3+4 (ages 33–72). Both children will eventually need their own histograms, to search for their own further splits.

1
Pick the smaller child to build directly. Left has 6 rows, Right has 6 rows here (a tie in this toy example — in practice one side is almost always smaller). Say we build Left's histogram by actually scanning its 6 rows: \(G_{\text{Left}}=-9\), \(H_{\text{Left}}=6\) (exactly the running total we already computed above).
2
Get Right's histogram for free — no row scanning at all. Since Parent = Left + Right for every bin, and we already know the Parent's total (\(G=0,H=12\)) from before this split ever happened:
\[ G_{\text{Right}}=G_{\text{Parent}}-G_{\text{Left}}=0-(-9)=9,\qquad H_{\text{Right}}=H_{\text{Parent}}-H_{\text{Left}}=12-6=6 \]
3
Verify: this matches exactly what we'd get by directly scanning Right's own rows (Bins 3+4: \(2+7=9\), \(3+3=6\)) — confirmed identical, but computed with zero additional row scans.
Why this compounds: at every single split, in every tree, LightGBM only ever pays the row-scanning cost for the smaller of the two new children. The larger child's histogram is pure subtraction — one array subtraction, per bin, no data access. Across a whole tree with many splits, this roughly halves the histogram-building work compared to naively rebuilding both children's histograms from scratch every time. Layer GOSS on top (§3) and the child that is built from scratch is built from a GOSS-reduced row set, not the full data — two independent savings, multiplying together.
05 · leaf-wise growth NEW

The most important difference: level-wise vs leaf-wise

Ask yourself: you already know how a single split is chosen. Now: in what order does a tree decide which node to split next?

Level-wise (depth-wise) — GBDT / XGBoost default

Root / \ A B / \ / \ C D E F

Every node at the current depth gets expanded before the tree goes deeper — A and B both split together, regardless of how much gain each actually offers.

Leaf-wise / best-first — LightGBM

Root / \ A B / \ C D

Suppose \(Gain(A)=12\), \(Gain(B)=4\). LightGBM splits whichever currently open leaf has the highest gain:

\[ \boxed{\text{split } A}\quad(\text{Gain}=12 > 4) \]

B stays a leaf, untouched, for now. Now suppose the next round of candidate gains are \(Gain(C)=2\), \(Gain(D)=15\), \(Gain(B)=4\):

\[ \boxed{\text{split } D}\quad(\text{Gain}=15\text{, the global best}\text{ — even though }D\text{ is deeper than }B) \]
The key intuition: leaf-wise never asks "whose turn is it at this depth." It asks "across every currently open leaf, anywhere in the tree, which one gives the single biggest improvement right now?" — and splits exactly that one.

07 · leaf-wise growth NEW

num_leaves vs max_depth: are these the same thing?

Ask yourself: our example above just grew 4 leaves at 2 different depths in the same tree. Does "depth" even mean the same thing here as it did for a level-wise tree?

max_depth

Caps the longest root-to-leaf path allowed anywhere in the tree.

num_leaves

Caps the total leaf count, directly — the actual unit leaf-wise growth spends its "budget" in.

Two trees, same num_leaves = 4, very different depth: BALANCED (level-wise-shaped) UNBALANCED (leaf-wise-typical) Root Root / \ / \ A B A B / \ / \ / \ C D E F ← depth 2, 4 leaves C D ← depth 3, 4 leaves / \ E F
Leaf-wise trees are not required to be balanced — exactly like our worked example, where Right kept splitting to depth 2 while Left sat untouched. A perfectly balanced tree gives \(num\_leaves\le2^{max\_depth}\), but leaf-wise routinely produces long, narrow branches that violate that bound's spirit — a handful of leaves at great depth, everywhere else shallow.
Do not blindly set \(num\_leaves=2^{max\_depth}\). That value assumes a balanced tree, which leaf-wise growth has no reason to produce. Setting num_leaves that high with an unconstrained max_depth is a common way to accidentally build very deep, overfit-prone trees — num_leaves is the primary lever in LightGBM; max_depth is usually a secondary safety cap.

09 · classification SAME

Classification: the underlying boosting math is unchanged

For binary log loss, everything you derived in the XGBoost chapter carries over exactly:

\[ p_i=\sigma(F_i),\qquad g_i=p_i-y_i,\qquad h_i=p_i(1-p_i) \]

Initial score, leaf weight, split Gain — all identical formulas. What changes for LightGBM is purely mechanism: histogram construction on \(g,h\), leaf-wise selection among currently open leaves, GOSS row sampling, EFB feature bundling, and native categorical splitting — layered on top of the same \(g,h\) you already know how to derive.

With the small, perfectly-separable 6-row toy dataset from the XGBoost chapter (\(x=1..6\), \(y=0,0,0,1,1,1\)), a single split at \(x\le3.5\) already separates the classes cleanly — there's no further-split gain left to compare, so it can't demonstrate leaf-wise growth meaningfully. Real classification datasets are never this clean, which is exactly why leaf-wise growth matters in practice; the comparison mechanism is identical to the regression leaf-wise walkthrough above — compare every open leaf's best candidate gain, split the global maximum, regardless of depth.
10 · GOSS NEW

GOSS: the problem, from scratch

Assume you know nothing about GOSS yet. Start from the computational reality: \(n=10{,}000{,}000\) rows. After computing every row's gradient, ask:

Ask yourself: are all 10 million rows equally useful for deciding the next split?

Compare two rows: one with \(|g_i|=0.01\), another with \(|g_i|=2.5\).

Large \(|g_i|\)

The current model is making a relatively large error on this row — it still needs a strong correction. This row carries a lot of information about where the model is wrong.

Small \(|g_i|\)

The model is already close to correct here. This row still matters (for coverage, for not overfitting the "hard" rows) — but it's less informative about which direction to correct next.

Large-gradient rows are disproportionately informative about where the next tree should focus. GOSS — Gradient-based One-Side Sampling — exploits exactly this asymmetry to use fewer rows per split search, without simply discarding the "easy" rows.
11 · GOSS NEW

The GOSS idea, name broken down

Gradient-based

Which rows to sample is decided by \(|g_i|\), nothing else.

One-Side

We keep one side outright — the large-gradient rows — and only sample from the other side (small-gradient rows). Not symmetric random sampling of everything.

Mechanically, with \(n=1000\):

1. Sort all rows by |g_i|, descending 2. Keep the top a = 20% outright → 200 rows, ALL of them, full weight 3. From the remaining 80% (800 rows), randomly sample b = 10% of THAT → 80 rows, reweighted 4. Total used for this split search: 200 + 80 = 280 rows (instead of 1000)
This is not a one-time filter on the dataset. It's recomputed every boosting round, on that round's current gradients — see §16.

13 · GOSS NEW

Why the reweighting factor has to exist

Ask yourself: if we only look at half of the small-gradient rows, but treat them exactly like the ones we kept, what happens to their total contribution to \(G,H\)?

It gets underestimated — by construction, we only observed \(b\) of the \((1-a)\) fraction of the population, so whatever those \(b\) rows sum to needs to be scaled back up to represent the whole \((1-a)\) group, not just the sampled sliver.

\[ \boxed{\text{reweight factor}=\frac{1-a}{b}} \]

Here: \(\dfrac{1-0.2}{0.5}=\dfrac{0.8}{0.5}=1.6\). Every sampled small-gradient row's \(g_i\) and \(h_i\) gets multiplied by 1.6 before entering any \(G,H\) sum — this keeps the GOSS sample's aggregated statistics an unbiased estimate of what the full small-gradient population would have contributed.

15 · GOSS NEW

GOSS across boosting iterations: it's not a one-time sample

Common misconception: GOSS is not "downsample the dataset once, then boost on that fixed subset." The gradients themselves change every round as the model updates — so which rows count as "large-gradient" changes too.

Continuing the 10-row example illustratively: after round 1's tree corrects the model, rows 1 and 2 (previously the largest errors) are now partly fixed, while other rows may have grown relatively larger residuals.

IterationTop-gradient rows (kept)Sampled small-gradient rowsEffective subset size
1{1, 2}{3, 4, 5, 6} (illustrative draw)6 of 10
2{2, 7} (gradients shifted after round 1's correction){1, 3, 8, 9} (new draw)6 of 10
3{7, 10} (shifted again){2, 4, 5, 9} (new draw)6 of 10
The mechanism is identical every round — recompute \(|g_i|\) on the current predictions, take the new top \(a\), resample \(b\) of the new remainder, reweight by \((1-a)/b\). Only the membership of "top" and "sampled" shifts as the model improves.
16 · GOSS NEW

When GOSS helps — and when it doesn't

Without GOSS

Every row scanned, every split, every tree. Maximum information, maximum cost.

With GOSS

Fewer rows scanned, prioritising the ones that matter most right now. Lower cost, an approximation of the full-data Gain.

DimensionEffect
Computation / split searchMeaningfully cheaper — fewer rows per histogram build
MemorySmaller working set per split search
Statistical approximationRanking of splits usually preserved well; exact Gain values are approximate
"Better" does not mean "universally more accurate." It means a better efficiency/accuracy trade-off specifically when \(n\) is large. On small datasets, the sampling noise can outweigh the speed benefit — GOSS is most valuable exactly where the original problem (millions of rows) lives.
17 · EFB NEW

EFB: why high-dimensional sparse data is a problem

Forget abstract feature names for a moment. Here is where this problem shows up constantly in real data: one-hot encoding.

Suppose you one-hot encode a "City" column with 1,000 distinct cities. You now have 1,000 brand-new columns: City_Chennai, City_Mumbai, City_Delhi, … For any single row, exactly one of these 1,000 columns is 1, and the other 999 are 0.

Row 1 (lives in Chennai): City_Chennai=1 City_Mumbai=0 City_Delhi=0 City_Bangalore=0 ... (997 more zeros) Row 2 (lives in Mumbai): City_Chennai=0 City_Mumbai=1 City_Delhi=0 City_Bangalore=0 ... (997 more zeros)
Ask yourself: to build a histogram for City_Chennai alone, you must scan every single row in the dataset — even though 999 times out of 1,000, that row is going to contribute a zero. Same for City_Mumbai. Same for all 1,000 columns. Is that not an enormous amount of wasted scanning?
EFB — Exclusive Feature Bundling — asks exactly this: since City_Chennai and City_Mumbai are never both non-zero on the same row (a person can't simultaneously "be" both Chennai and Mumbai in a one-hot column), can we merge many of these columns into one, so we only ever build one histogram instead of a thousand?

This is the single biggest source of the "1,000,000 sparse features" scenario in practice — one-hot encoded categoricals, bag-of-words text features, and rare interaction features all produce exactly this pattern: many columns, almost all zero, rarely non-zero together.

19 · EFB NEW

How bundling actually works — every step, no skipping

Ask yourself, first: since A and B never overlap, why not just create one new column that's simply \(A+B\)?
Because addition destroys identity. Suppose Feature A can take bin-value 2, and Feature B can also take bin-value 2 (on a different row, since they're exclusive). If we compute \(A+B\), a row where \(A{=}2,B{=}0\) gives bundle value 2. A different row where \(A{=}0,B{=}2\) also gives bundle value 2. The split-finder now sees two identical bundle values that actually came from two completely different features — it has no way to tell them apart anymore. This is the exact problem we must solve.

Step 1 — give each feature its own private "address range" (the offset)

Think of it like a building with numbered floors. Feature A gets floors 0–3 all to itself. Feature B gets floors 4–7, all to itself. As long as the floor ranges never overlap, you can always tell which "tenant" (which original feature) a floor number belongs to, just by looking at the number.

Feature A's own bins: 0, 1, 2, 3 ← occupies range [0, 3] Feature B's own bins: 0, 1, 2, 3 +4 offset → becomes 4, 5, 6, 7 ← occupies range [4, 7]
The "+4" is called the offset. It's just Feature A's own bin count (4 bins → offset of 4) added onto every one of Feature B's bin values, so B's numbers start exactly where A's numbers end.

Step 2 — build the bundle value, row by row (all 5 rows, fully worked)

Rule for each row: if A is non-zero, the bundle value is just A's own bin. If A is zero, look at B instead — and if B is non-zero, the bundle value is B's bin PLUS the offset (4). If both are zero, the bundle value is 0 (nothing active).

RowA (bin)B (bin)Which is non-zero?Bundle value

One merged column, 5 rows: [2, 5, 3, 7, 0]. That's the entire bundle — a single array of numbers, built once.

Step 3 — decoding: recovering which feature owned each value

To read a bundle value back, just check which range it falls in — this is the whole reason the offset had to be big enough to keep ranges disjoint:

Nothing is lost. The split-finder now builds and scans one histogram over this single bundled column instead of two separate (mostly-empty) ones — and can still fully recover, for any candidate split, exactly which original feature and which of its bins is on each side.

Conflicts: when exclusivity isn't perfect

Real features are rarely perfectly exclusive. Modify one row of our original A/B example:

RowAB
110
202
330
404
557
Ask yourself: row 5 now has both A and B non-zero at once. If we still force them into one bundle, what actually breaks?

Our Step 2 rule from §19 said "if A is non-zero, use A's bin; only look at B if A is zero." So for row 5, the bundle would record only A's value (bin for 5) — B's value (7) for that row is silently dropped. That row now behaves, to the split-finder, as if Feature B were 0 there, which is simply wrong.

This is a conflict: a specific row where two "supposedly exclusive" features are simultaneously non-zero, forcing the bundling rule to lose one of the two values.
Conflict rate, with a bigger worked example

Suppose across 20 rows, exactly 2 rows have both A and B non-zero at once:

\[ \text{conflict rate}=\frac{\#\text{ rows with a conflict}}{\#\text{ total rows}}=\frac{2}{20}=0.10=10\% \]
LightGBM allows bundling two features together as long as their conflict rate stays under a chosen tolerance (a max-conflict-rate setting). Allow more conflicts → more aggressive, more compressed bundling, but slightly wrong statistics on those specific conflicting rows. Allow fewer → safer, but less compression. In practice, genuinely sparse one-hot-style features have a conflict rate at or near exactly 0%, so this is rarely a hard trade-off for the classic use case — it starts to matter more for less cleanly sparse features.
21 · EFB NEW

EFB as a graph problem: why graph colouring shows up

With thousands of features, "which ones are safe to bundle together" isn't a single yes/no question — it's a genuine combinatorial puzzle. Here's how to represent it as a graph, one piece at a time.

1
Every feature becomes a dot (a "node"). If you have 5 features A, B, C, D, E, you draw 5 dots.
2
Draw a line ("edge") between two dots exactly when those two features conflict too often to bundle (their conflict rate is above your tolerance — often meaning "they're ever non-zero on the same row at all," for the strict case).
3
No line between two dots means those two features are safe to put in the same bundle.
A —— C (A and C conflict — cannot share a bundle) B —— D (B and D conflict — cannot share a bundle) E (E conflicts with nobody — free to join any bundle)
Ask yourself: we now want to sort every dot into groups ("bundles"), such that no two dots connected by a line ever land in the same group. Where exactly have you seen this problem before, under a different name?
Graph colouring. Give every node a "colour" (think: a bundle ID, like Bundle-1, Bundle-2, …) such that no two nodes joined by an edge ever share a colour. The goal is to use as few colours as possible — fewer colours means fewer final bundles, which means more compression. Finding the true minimum is NP-hard in general, so LightGBM uses a fast, practical greedy colouring heuristic instead of solving it exactly (worked by hand next, in §22).
Raw sparse features ↓ Build the conflict graph (node = feature, edge = "too conflicting to bundle") ↓ Greedy graph colouring (assign each node the lowest-numbered colour that avoids its neighbours) ↓ Each colour = one final feature bundle
Reducing, say, 10,000 sparse features down to roughly 2,000 bundles is exactly this mechanism running at scale — not a fixed compression ratio anyone chose, but simply the natural outcome of however sparse and however mutually exclusive the real feature set happens to be.

How native categorical splitting actually works

Ask yourself: with \(K\) categories, there are up to \(2^{K-1}-1\) meaningfully different ways to partition them into two groups. Do we really enumerate all of them?

No. LightGBM orders the categories by a relevant statistic, then only searches thresholds along that ordering — exactly like a numeric split, but on a smart, data-driven order instead of an arbitrary one.

The ordering statistic

For each category value, aggregate its rows' gradient and Hessian sums, then rank categories by:

\[ \text{stat}(\text{category})=\frac{\sum_{i\in\text{category}} g_i}{\sum_{i\in\text{category}} h_i} \]
This is precisely the un-regularised optimal leaf value shape you already know (\(-G/H\)) — categories are sorted by "how much correction this category alone would want," from most-negative to most-positive. Categories that behave similarly end up adjacent in the ordering.
Then: instead of enumerating every subset, LightGBM only tries the \(K-1\) contiguous splits along this single ordering — "first \(k\) categories in this order vs the rest," exactly the same prefix-sum mechanism as a numeric histogram split. \(K-1\) candidates, not \(2^{K-1}-1\).

Is this just target encoding, then?

Ask yourself: "order categories by a target-related statistic" sounds a lot like target encoding. Are they the same thing?

Related in spirit, but genuinely different in mechanism. Here's the precise distinction, side by side:

Target encodingLightGBM native categorical
What gets computedOne scalar per category (e.g. mean target), from the whole training set (usually with smoothing/out-of-fold to fight leakage)An ordering statistic \(G/H\), from only the rows currently at this specific tree node
Is it saved as a feature?Yes — becomes a permanent new numeric column, reused everywhere, every tree, every roundNo — the ordering is recomputed fresh, purely to decide scan order for this one split search, then discarded
What does it use as the target?The raw label \(y\) (fixed, from the original data)The current gradient \(g\) and Hessian \(h\) — which change every boosting round as the model updates
Does the order ever change?No — fixed once the encoding is computedYes — can differ at every node and every round, since it depends on which rows reached that node and what the model currently gets wrong there
Resulting split typeAn ordinary numeric threshold split on the encoded scalar columnA genuine categorical partition — explicit subsets of category IDs on each side
The honest one-line summary: target encoding permanently replaces a category with a number and then treats it exactly like any other numeric feature. LightGBM's native handling never replaces anything — the category IDs stay categorical throughout; a target/gradient-based statistic is only borrowed, briefly and locally, to decide a sensible order to scan candidate partitions in, and that borrowed order can be completely different at the next node or the next tree.
Because target encoding bakes a fixed, whole-dataset statistic into a persistent feature, it's genuinely leakage-prone without careful regularisation (smoothing, out-of-fold computation). LightGBM's native approach is less exposed to that specific failure mode — each ordering is recomputed from the small, local set of rows at that node using current gradients — though rare categories with very few rows at a node can still produce a noisy, unstable order, which is exactly what cat_smooth exists to dampen.

28 · categorical features NEW

The ordinal-encoding trap, precisely

Low = 0 Medium = 1 High = 2
Ask yourself: does LightGBM know these are categories just because they're small integers?

Not automatically. If this column is passed as an ordinary numeric feature, \(0<1<2\) is taken at face value — the model assumes Medium sits "between" Low and High in whatever sense the split search cares about.

If instead this column is explicitly marked categorical (via dtype or categorical_feature), LightGBM switches to the ordering-by-statistic search from §26 — it may still discover that Low < Medium < High behaves sensibly (often true for genuinely ordinal data like this), but it derives that from the data's actual gradient behaviour, not from assuming it upfront.
For genuinely ordinal categories (Low/Medium/High), passing them as plain numeric 0/1/2 is often fine in practice, precisely because the "natural" order usually does align with outcome behaviour. The trap is for nominal categories with no true order (city names, product IDs) — there, an arbitrary integer code can silently mislead a numeric split search into inventing an order that doesn't exist.

GOSS hyperparameters, especially deep

Recall from §12: \(top\_rate=0.2\), \(other\_rate=0.1\) means keep the top 20% by \(|g|\) outright, then sample 10% of the remaining 80%.

ChangeEffect
top_rate ↑More large-gradient rows kept outright — closer to full-data accuracy, less speed benefit
top_rate ↓Fewer rows guaranteed kept — faster, risk of missing genuinely important high-error rows
other_rate ↑More of the "easy" rows sampled — better small-gradient coverage, slower
other_rate ↓Sparser small-gradient coverage — faster, more sampling noise in the reweighted contribution
The trade-off is always the same shape: higher rates → closer to the exact full-data Gain, slower; lower rates → cheaper, noisier approximation. \(top\_rate\) protects against missing the rows that matter most; \(other\_rate\) protects against systematically ignoring the "easy" majority.
33 · tuning

Underfitting vs overfitting, LightGBM-specific

Leaf-wise growth can overfit faster than level-wise growth for the same leaf count — because it always spends its next split where the *training* loss improves most, it can carve out very deep, narrow, thinly-supported branches chasing a handful of outlier rows, while a level-wise tree spreads the same leaf budget evenly and stays shallower everywhere.
Level-wise (same leaf budget, spread evenly): Leaf-wise (same budget, can go very deep): root root / \ / \ A B A B / \ / \ / \ C D / \ E F

Underfitting

  • num_leaves too low
  • min_data_in_leaf / min_sum_hessian_in_leaf too high
  • min_gain_to_split too high
  • lambda_l1 / lambda_l2 too high
  • learning_rate too small with too few trees

Overfitting

  • num_leaves too high (the #1 lever here)
  • max_depth unconstrained alongside high num_leaves
  • min_data_in_leaf too low
  • feature_fraction / bagging_fraction too high (no stochastic dampening)
  • learning_rate too high, too many trees, no early stopping

LightGBM vs XGBoost, precisely — not just "faster"

DimensionXGBoostLightGBM
Tree growthLevel-wise / depth-wiseLeaf-wise / best-first

Row samplingUniform row/column subsamplingGOSS (gradient-prioritised) + ordinary bagging

Why "LightGBM is faster," precisely: leaf-wise growth reduces wasted splits on low-gain nodes; GOSS reduces the number of rows scanned per split; EFB reduces the number of effectively-distinct sparse features scanned; native categorical search avoids one-hot dimensionality blow-ups. Four independent multipliers on cost, not one single trick.
Speed is not free. Leaf-wise growth's overfitting risk (§33) and GOSS's approximation noise are the direct costs of those speed gains — which is why num_leaves, min_data_in_leaf, and validation-driven early stopping matter more here than they typically do for a level-wise XGBoost model.

38 · wrap-up

Hyperparameter cheat sheet

If overfitting…

  • decrease num_leaves — directly cuts capacity, the single biggest lever
  • decrease max_depth — caps runaway deep branches
  • increase min_data_in_leaf — forces leaves to have real support
  • increase min_sum_hessian_in_leaf — forces real curvature evidence
  • increase min_gain_to_split — makes every split earn its keep
  • increase lambda_l1 / lambda_l2 — shrinks/zeroes leaf weights
  • decrease feature_fraction / bagging_fraction — adds stochastic dampening
  • decrease learning_rate, use early stopping — more cautious, validation-driven steps

If underfitting…

  • increase num_leaves — more capacity
  • decrease min_data_in_leaf / min_sum_hessian_in_leaf — allow finer leaves
  • decrease min_gain_to_split — allow more marginal splits
  • decrease lambda_l1 / lambda_l2 — let weights move further
  • increase learning_rate or n_estimators — more total correction
Every one of these works because of the mechanism you derived earlier in this chapter, not by coincidence — e.g. num_leaves directly caps how many times leaf-wise growth is allowed to chase the next-best gain; min_gain_to_split is literally the γ-style threshold from the Gain formula.
