Step 1 · Foundations

Polynomial regression — transformed features

So far our model has looked like ŷ = w₀ + w₁x — a straight line. But real relationships are often curved. Polynomial regression's trick is simple: we don't change the model, we change the input. We create new columns out of x, and then fit an ordinary (still linear!) equation on top of those new columns.

Step-by-step: turning one feature into many

1 Start with a single feature x, with values 1, 2, 3, 4.
2 Create a new column x² by squaring every value: 1, 4, 9, 16.
3 Create another new column x³ by cubing every value: 1, 8, 27, 64.
4 Now treat x, x², x³ as three separate, ordinary input columns and fit a normal linear regression on top of them.

This still looks exactly like ordinary multiple linear regression — because it is one! We simply feed it engineered columns instead of raw ones. That's the whole trick behind "polynomial" regression: the *model* stays linear in its weights, only the *features* get curvier.

Degree 1 → higher degree: what actually changes

"Degree" just means: how many of these power-columns (x, x², x³, ... xᵈ) you include.

📏

Degree 1

ŷ = w₀+w₁x — a straight line. Can only ever tilt, never bend.

〰️

Degree 2–4

ŷ = w₀+w₁x+w₂x²+... — can bend once or twice, like a gentle hill or valley.

🐍

Degree 15+

Can wiggle back and forth many times — enough to snake through every single training point.

In your notes: a 10-feature dataset expanded to degree 8 stops being "10 features" — it balloons into every power and cross-term up to degree 8. More degree = more columns = more weights = a hugely more flexible (and hungrier) model.

Occam's razor — simplicity as a tie-breaker

Occam's razor is an old philosophical idea, and it turns out to be exactly the right instinct for machine learning too: "among models that explain the data roughly equally well, prefer the simpler one."

Why this matters for degree-choice

Concretely: from your notes, comparing a degree-2 model against a degree-8 model on the same data —

modeldegreescore
M₁274%
M₂878%
M₂ scores a bit higher — but is 4 extra points of accuracy worth 6 extra degrees of complexity (many more weights, far more ways to memorize noise)? Occam's razor says: not necessarily. A slightly-lower-scoring simple model is often the wiser real-world choice, because — as we're about to see — the complex one's high score can be an illusion.
Step 2 · The core problem

Underfitting — the model is too lazy

Underfitting happens when the model is too simple to capture the real pattern in the data. Think of it as a student who barely skimmed the textbook — they do badly even on the practice questions they've already seen.

A self-learn example

Suppose the true relationship between x and y is actually a curve: y = x². But you train a plain straight line, ŷ = mx + b, on this data. No matter how you rotate that line, it can never bend to match a curve — so it does poorly even on the training points themselves.
Tell-tale sign of underfitting: training error is already high. The model fails the "practice test" too, not just the real one. If your training accuracy itself is disappointing, don't reach for regularization — reach for a more expressive model (higher degree, more features) instead.

Overfitting — the model is memorizing, not learning

Overfitting is the opposite failure: the model is so flexible that instead of learning the general pattern, it bends itself to pass through every single training point — including the random noise and outliers that shouldn't matter.

A self-learn example, with real numbers from class

A high-degree model was fit on training data, and scored:

metricR² score
Train R²0.99
Test R²0.72
This gap is the fingerprint of overfitting: training error is very low, but test error is much higher. The model looks brilliant on data it has already memorized, and mediocre on new data it hasn't seen — exactly like a student who memorized last year's question paper word-for-word, but can't solve a rephrased version of the same question.
Visually (from the board): the green wiggly curve threads tightly through every noisy training dot — that's overfitting in action. A much smoother curve would miss a few training points slightly, but track the true underlying pattern far better.

The Bias–Variance trade-off — made simple

This is just a more precise vocabulary for the same underfitting/overfitting story. Two words, two very different kinds of "being wrong":

🎯

Bias

How far off, on average, the model's assumptions are from reality. A straight line assuming a curved world = high bias.

🌪️

Variance

How much the model's predictions swing around if you retrain it on a slightly different sample of data. A model that memorizes noise = high variance.

The dartboard picture — the easiest way to remember this

Imagine throwing darts at a bullseye, many times over. Bias = how far off-center the darts land on average. Variance = how spread out the darts are from each other.

Low bias + Low variance — tight cluster, right on the bullseye. The goal.
Low bias + High variance overfitting — scattered, but centered on average
High bias + Low variance underfitting — tight cluster, but off-target
High bias + High variance — scattered and off-target. Worst of both.
A simple model = darts clustered tightly, but clustered in the wrong spot (high bias, low variance = underfitting). A complex model = darts scattered around the right spot (low bias, high variance = overfitting). We want both errors small at once — top-left target — that's the trade-off.

Why it's called a "trade-off"

As you move from a simple model (degree 1) to a very complex one (degree 15):

model complexitybiasvariance
Low (degree 1)HighLow
Medium (degree ~4)BalancedBalanced
High (degree 15)LowHigh
Total Error = Bias² + Variance + Noise
"Noise" is the irreducible error — randomness in the real world that no model, however good, can ever predict away (e.g. outliers, measurement error).
As model complexity increases, bias keeps falling but variance keeps rising. Total error is shaped like a U — it's minimized somewhere in the middle, not at either extreme. Finding that sweet spot is the entire goal of this class.
Step 3 · The fix

Why big weights are the actual villain

Here's the missing link: why does a high-degree polynomial overfit? Concretely, because it's allowed to assign huge, wild weights to the higher-power columns (x⁵, x⁸, ...) in order to bend and twist through every noisy point. Large weights = large sensitivity = tiny changes in input swing the prediction wildly. That sensitivity is variance.

What Is Regularization?

The idea: add a penalty to the loss function that punishes large weights, so the model has two competing goals instead of one.

Loss (plain MSE) = (1/n) × Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²
Regularized Loss = MSE + λ × Penalty(weights)

Goal 1

Minimize prediction error — fit the training data.

Goal 2

Keep all weights small — stay simple, don't memorise.

λ (lambda) is a hyperparameter — you set it, the model doesn't learn it by itself. It controls how much you care about goal 2 relative to goal 1. λ = 0 → the penalty vanishes and the model learns entirely on its own (can overfit freely). λ → very large → the penalty dominates so much that weights get crushed toward zero regardless of the data (underfitting).

Two ways to define "Penalty(weights)"

L2 · Ridge

Penalty = Σ wᵢ² (sum of squared weights)

L1 · Lasso

Penalty = Σ |wᵢ| (sum of absolute weights)

Squaring vs. taking absolute value sounds like a tiny detail — it is actually the entire reason these two behave completely differently, which is exactly what we derive next.

Ridge Regression L2

Ridge adds λ·w² to the loss for every weight. Let's watch the weight actually update during gradient descent, with one concrete weight.

The full Ridge loss, and its gradient

Lridge(w) = MSE(w) + λ·w²
1 Differentiate the penalty term alone: d/dw[λw²] = 2λw. (Ordinary power rule — bring the 2 down, drop the power by one.)
2 So the full gradient of the regularized loss is: (gradient of plain MSE) + 2λw.
3 Gradient descent's weight-update rule stays exactly the same shape as always:
wnew = wold − η · [ gradient of MSE + 2λwold ]
η (eta) is the learning rate — how big a step we take at each round.
Read the extra term 2λw as a constant "tax" proportional to the current weight itself. The bigger w already is, the bigger the pull back toward 0 — but the pull shrinks as w shrinks. This is exactly a spring's restoring force (Hooke's law: force ∝ displacement). It gets weaker and weaker near 0, but mathematically it never becomes exactly zero for a finite w. That's why Ridge shrinks weights smoothly, but essentially never lands exactly on 0.

Worked numeric example

Data: x=1→y=5, x=2→y=10 (so unpenalized fit gives exactly w=5). Full derivation:

L(w) = (5−w)² + (10−2w)² = 125 − 50w + 5w²
Lridge(w) = 125 − 50w + 5w² + λw² = 125 − 50w + (5+λ)w²
L′(w) = −50 + 2(5+λ)w = 0  →  w = 25 / (5+λ)

Notice: however large λ gets, w = (something) ÷ (a positive number) — it can get microscopically close to 0, but a finite λ can never make it land exactly on 0. That's the spring never fully letting go.

Lasso Regression L1

Lasso adds λ·|w| instead. The moment you switch from w² to |w|, the derivative stops being smooth — and that single change is what allows Lasso to push weights to exactly zero (and thereby do automatic feature selection).

The gradient of the penalty term

Llasso(w) = MSE(w) + λ·|w|
1 For w > 0: |w| = w, so its slope is a constant +1.
2 For w < 0: |w| = −w, so its slope is a constant −1.
3 So the penalty's gradient is always ±λ — a fixed size, regardless of how big or small w currently is.
wnew = wold − η · [ gradient of MSE ± λ ]
Read ±λ as a constant friction force — same strength whether w is huge or tiny, always pushing toward 0. Unlike a spring, friction doesn't taper off as you approach zero — so once the data's own pull is weaker than friction's pull, the weight gets stuck exactly at 0 and stays there. That's the wall Ridge never has.
⭐ Optional deep-dive

Why does Lasso actually hit exactly 0, while Ridge never does?

This section is optional but highly recommended — it's the full worked proof behind the one-liner above, using the same x=1→y=5, x=2→y=10 example.

The physical picture to hold in your head

Ridge = a spring

Pull force ∝ distance from zero. Strong pull far away, gentle pull close in — but never fully "runs out" of pull. Force = 2λw.

Lasso = friction against a wall

A constant resistance force, same size no matter the distance — until the weight reaches the wall (zero) and gets stuck there. Force = λ·sign(w).

Setting up the exact same loss

Llasso(w) = 125 − 50w + 5w² + λ|w|

Because |w| behaves differently depending on the sign of w, we must check three separate cases — we can't just differentiate through the corner.

Case 1 — assume w > 0

L(w) = 125 − 50w + 5w² + λw
L′(w) = −50 + 10w + λ = 0  →  w = 5 − λ/10
Only valid while this comes out positive (matching our assumption): 5 − λ/10 > 0 → λ < 50.

Case 2 — assume w < 0

L(w) = 125 − (50+λ)w + 5w²  →  L′(w)=0  →  w = 5 + λ/10
Only valid if this is negative: 5+λ/10 < 0 → λ < −50. Since λ (a penalty strength) can never be negative, this case never triggers for this data — the data itself already wants a positive weight (+5), and the penalty can only pull toward zero, never manufacture a pull the data never asked for.

Case 3 — the tricky one: w = 0

At exactly w=0, |w| has no single slope. Instead of differentiating, we ask: is there some legal slope, from the allowed range [−1, +1], that makes the total slope exactly zero?

1 Smooth part's own slope at w=0: differentiate 125−50w+5w² → −50+10w → at w=0, this is simply −50.
2 The corner part's slope at w=0 can be any value s in [−1,+1] — call it the "wildcard" slope.
3 λ is just a constant multiplier, so it rides straight through differentiation untouched: derivative of λ|w| at w=0 is λ·s.
4 Add both pieces, set the total to zero (the flat condition every minimum needs):
−50 + λ·s = 0  →  s = 50/λ
5 This s is only legal if it stays inside [−1, +1]:
50/λ ≤ 1  →  λ ≥ 50
Sanity check via left/right slopes directly (no subgradient trick needed):
Right side (w→0⁺): slope = −50+λ. For the loss to not decrease moving right, need −50+λ ≥ 0 → λ ≥ 50.
Left side (w→0⁻): slope = −50−λ, which is always negative for λ≥0 — so moving left never helps, no constraint needed there.
Both routes agree: λ ≥ 50 is exactly when w=0 stops being "downhill" in every direction — i.e. exactly when 0 truly is the minimum.

Check the handoff at λ=50: Case 1 gives w = 5−50/10 = 0 exactly, matching Case 3's boundary perfectly — no gap, no contradiction.

Collapsing the two live cases into one formula: max()

Push λ past 50 in Case 1's raw formula anyway: at λ=60, "5−λ/10" = −1. But we proved w can never actually be negative for this data. So the raw formula becomes "wrong" past λ=50 — the real answer just freezes at 0 instead of following it down. "Never let a number go below 0" is exactly what max(x, 0) means:

The one line to remember

Ridge is a spring — always some pull left, so it shrinks weights forever without fully deleting them. Lasso is friction against a wall — a constant pull that can fully stop a weight exactly at zero, and once stopped, it stays stopped. Same goal (discourage large weights), opposite behavior, purely from the shape of the penalty: smooth curve (w²) vs. sharp corner (|w|).

Elastic Net — why choose, when you can blend?

Ridge is great at handling correlated features smoothly, but never eliminates any. Lasso is great at automatic feature selection, but can behave erratically when features are highly correlated (it tends to arbitrarily pick just one of them and zero the rest). Elastic Net's idea: use both penalties at once, with a knob to control the mix.

The formula

Lelastic(w) = MSE(w) + λ₁·|w| + λ₂·w²
Some libraries write this with one λ for overall strength and a mixing ratio α ∈ [0,1]: L = MSE + λ[ α·|w| + (1−α)·w² ]. α=1 is pure Lasso, α=0 is pure Ridge, anything in between is a blend.

α close to 1

Mostly Lasso behavior — sparser, more weights hit exactly 0.

α close to 0

Mostly Ridge behavior — smoother shrinkage, few or no exact zeros.

Intuition: Lasso's "friction" gives you sparsity (some weights truly gone — useful when you suspect only a handful of features actually matter). Ridge's "spring" gives you stability with correlated features (it spreads the penalty across all of them instead of arbitrarily picking one). Elastic Net lets you dial in some of both, instead of being forced to pick a single extreme.
Step 4 · Picking λ properly

So... how do we actually choose λ?

We now know λ controls the whole story — too small and we overfit, too large and we underfit. But λ is a hyperparameter: the model can't learn it from the training data (training data will always "prefer" λ=0, since that lets it fit itself best!). We need a fair, honest way to test different λ values. That's what validation data is for.

The Three-Way Split

Every ML project divides its data into three non-overlapping sets:

Training Set
(60–70%)

The model sees this data and adjusts its weights to minimise error. This is where all the "learning" happens.

Validation Set
(10–20%)

Used to tune hyperparameters like λ. The model never trains on this — but you use it to decide which λ works best.

Test Set
(10–20%)

The final, honest evaluation. Touched exactly once, at the very end. Tells you how the model will really perform.

Worked example, matching class numbers

Say we have 1000 rows total. A common split: 700 for training, 100 for validation, 200 held back for testing.

splitrowsjob
Train700fit the model's weights, for a given λ
Validation100evaluate + compare across λ values, pick the winner
Test200final honest score, used only once, at the end
1 Try λ = 0.01 → train on the 700, check score on the 100 validation rows.
2 Try λ = 0.1, then 1, then 10, then 100 → same routine each time.
3 Whichever λ scores best on validation (not training!) is your chosen λ.
4 Only now do you touch the 200 test rows — once — to report the model's real-world performance.
Why not just pick λ using the test set directly? Because if you peek at test performance while tuning, you're indirectly "training" on the test set through your own choices — and your final reported score becomes overly optimistic and untrustworthy. The test set's entire value comes from never being used for decisions.

K-Fold Cross Validation — making validation more reliable

A single train/validation split has a weakness: what if, by pure luck, an easy or unusually hard chunk of data ended up in your one validation set? Your λ-comparison could be misleading. K-Fold cross-validation fixes this by validating multiple times, on different slices, and averaging the results.

How 5-fold cross-validation works, step by step

Take your training+validation pool (say 1000 rows) and split it into 5 equal chunks: D₁, D₂, D₃, D₄, D₅.

roundfold 1fold 2fold 3fold 4fold 5
Round 1VALtraintraintraintrain
Round 2trainVALtraintraintrain
Round 3traintrainVALtraintrain
Round 4traintraintrainVALtrain
Round 5traintraintraintrainVAL
Final Score = (Score₁ + Score₂ + Score₃ + Score₄ + Score₅) / 5
Every single row gets used for validation exactly once, and for training 4 times, across the 5 rounds. This means your final score reflects performance averaged over the whole dataset, not just whichever chunk happened to land in "the" validation set.

How much does the model actually execute? (the cost of doing this properly)

This is the detail that trips people up: cross-validation multiplies your training runs by however many λ values you're testing.

1 Say you want to test 3 candidate λ values: 0.1, 1, 10.
2 For each λ, you run the full 5-fold routine — that's 5 separate training runs.
3 Total training runs = (number of λ values) × (number of folds) = 3 × 5 = 15.
4 If you instead used K=10 folds: 3 × 10 = 30 total runs — twice the compute, for a more reliable (larger, more stable) average per λ.
value of Kbest fortrade-off
K = 5Default choice, most commonGood balance of speed and reliability
K = 10Smaller datasets~2× slower than K=5, but more reliable
K = N (Leave-One-Out)Very small datasets (<50 rows)Trains N separate models — extremely slow for large N
Stratified K-FoldImbalanced classification datasetsPreserves the class ratio in every fold — always use for classification
Whichever λ has the best average score across all folds is your winner. Then — and only then — you retrain one final model (using that λ) on all of your training+validation data combined, and evaluate it once on the untouched test set.
