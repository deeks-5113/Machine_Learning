These two shapes are exactly what we need — and remarkably, they fall directly out of maximum likelihood, which is what we derive next.

Deriving Log Loss from likelihood, step by step

Step 1 — the likelihood of a single point

"Likelihood" asks: given our model's probability pᵢ, how likely was the label we actually observed? Write it as one compact expression that works for both yᵢ=0 and yᵢ=1:

Lᵢ = pᵢyᵢ · (1−pᵢ)(1−yᵢ)
check yᵢ=1 Lᵢ = pᵢ¹·(1−pᵢ)⁰ = pᵢ — the likelihood is just pᵢ itself. Makes sense.
check yᵢ=0 Lᵢ = pᵢ⁰·(1−pᵢ)¹ = 1−pᵢ — the likelihood is 1−pᵢ. Also makes sense.

Step 2 — likelihood of the whole dataset, and why we want to MAXIMIZE it

Assuming rows are independent, the likelihood of seeing all the labels we actually observed is the product of each row's likelihood. We want weights that make our actual data look as likely as possible — so this is a maximize problem:

maximize   L = ∏ᵢ pᵢyᵢ(1−pᵢ)(1−yᵢ)

Step 3 — take the log (products are painful, sums are easy)

Log turns the product into a sum, and since log is increasing, whatever maximizes L also maximizes log(L):

maximize   log(L) = Σᵢ [ yᵢ·log(pᵢ) + (1−yᵢ)·log(1−pᵢ) ]

Step 4 — flip to a "loss" by negating and averaging

"Maximize log-likelihood" and "minimize negative log-likelihood" are the exact same problem — negating just relabels it into the minimize-a-loss language every other model uses:

Log Loss  =  −(1/n) Σᵢ [ yᵢ·log(pᵢ) + (1−yᵢ)·log(1−pᵢ) ]
This is exactly the −log(pᵢ) / −log(1−pᵢ) shapes from the table above — now derived properly from maximum likelihood, not just pattern-matched from a behavioral wishlist.

Why not MSE?

Two completely separate reasons — one about how errors get punished, one about the shape of the loss surface itself.

Reason 1 — with respect to errors: MSE punishes the wrong way

true ypredicted pMSE = (y−p)²Log Loss
10.990.00010.01
10.500.250.69
10.100.812.30
10.01≈1.0 (capped)4.61 (still climbing)
MSE's penalty is bounded — it can never exceed 1. A prediction of p=0.01 for a true label y=1 (confidently, badly wrong) barely costs more than p=0.1. Log Loss keeps growing without limit — it correctly treats "confidently wrong" as a much more serious mistake. MSE simply doesn't punish the worst errors hard enough.

Reason 2 — convexity: MSE+sigmoid is not convex, Log Loss is

Gradient descent needs a smooth, differentiable, well-behaved bowl to reliably find the best weights. Composing squared error with a sigmoid breaks that shape:

MSE + sigmoid

Non-Convex

Multiple dips and flat plateaus possible. Gradient descent can get stuck in a different, worse answer depending on where it starts.

Log Loss

Convex

One single bowl, one global minimum. No matter where gradient descent starts, it converges to the exact same best answer.

This is precisely why the loss function needed to be derived (from likelihood) rather than just reused from linear regression — the choice of loss isn't a style preference, it directly determines whether gradient descent can be trusted to find the right answer.
Keeping it generalizable

Regularization — what does C do in sklearn?

Log Loss alone keeps rewarding the model for pushing predictions ever closer to 0 or 1. Left unchecked, weights can grow without bound. Regularization adds a penalty to stop that — and sklearn controls its strength via C, not λ directly.

C = 1 / λ
directionλeffect on the penaltyresult
C → ∞  (λ → 0)≈ 0no penalty at alloverfitting risk
C → 0  (λ → ∞)very largeheavy penaltyunderfitting risk
Say it twice, it's easy to get backwards: small C means strong regularization (big effective λ); large C means weak regularization (tiny effective λ). It's the opposite direction from how λ itself reads.

Why sklearn writes it this way

Internally, sklearn's objective is C·Σ(LogLoss) + ½‖w‖² — C scales the loss term, not the penalty. Divide through by C and you recover the familiar Σloss + λ‖w‖² shape with λ≈1/C. Same idea as Ridge, just parameterized from the opposite side.

Impact of outliers on logistic regression

We already have the tool to measure this precisely — the loss function itself. Let's see how a single outlier's contribution to the loss changes depending on which side of the boundary it lands on.

L = −[ y·log(ŷ) + (1−y)·log(1−ŷ) ]

Say the outlier's true label is y=1. Then the (1−y)log(1−ŷ) term vanishes, leaving just:

L = −log(ŷ)

Case 1 — outlier on the correct side

The outlier sits far out, but on its own (correct) side of the boundary — z is significantly large and positive (say z=8).

ŷ = σ(8)≈ 0.9997
L = −log(0.9997)≈ 0.0003
Very low impact! The outlier is far from the cluster, but not far on the wrong side — it's still deep in class 1's own territory. The loss it contributes is almost exactly 0, so the fitted boundary has no reason to move.

Case 2 — outlier on the wrong side

The same class-1 outlier, but now sitting far into class 0's territory — misclassified, at a large distance. z is large and negative (say z=−4.3).

ŷ = σ(−4.3)≈ 0.0134
L = −log(0.0134)≈ 4.31
Very high impact! One point, sitting deep inside class 0's territory while actually belonging to class 1, contributes as much loss as dozens of correctly-classified points combined. To reduce that huge single term, training visibly rotates the boundary ("before" → "after") toward the outlier — cutting into what used to be a clean separation of the main clusters.

The takeaway

An outlier's danger has nothing to do with how far it sits from the rest of the data — it's entirely about which side of the boundary it lands on. Correctly-classified outliers are nearly harmless (the sigmoid saturates, capping their loss contribution). Misclassified outliers are extremely costly, and can noticeably drag the decision boundary toward them as the model tries to reduce that one point's huge loss contribution.

Odds & log-odds

Deriving odds and log-odds, properly

What "odds" means, concretely

Probability answers "what's the chance?" Odds answers a different question: "how many times more likely is this than the alternative?"

Odds = P / (1−P)
Example: P=0.80 (80% chance of buying) → Odds = 0.8/0.2 = 4 → "buying is 4 times more likely than not buying."

Going backward: given odds, recover P

This is a clean bit of algebra worth doing slowly:

1 P/(1−P) = odds
2 P = (1−P)·odds  (multiply both sides by 1−P)
3 P = odds − P·odds  (expand the right side)
4 P + P·odds = odds  (collect P terms on the left)
5 P(1+odds) = odds  (factor out P)
P = odds / (1+odds)
Check: odds=4 → P=4/5=0.8 ✓ matches where we started.

From odds to log-odds — and back to the sigmoid

We already know z = w₀+w₁x₁+… lives on (−∞,+∞), same as log(odds). Setting them equal:

log( P/(1−P) ) = z

Solve for P using the exact same style of algebra as above:

P/(1−P) = ez  →  P = ez(1−P)  →  P(1+ez) = ez
P = ez/(1+ez) = 1/(1+e−z) = σ(z)
This is the exact same sigmoid from Class 07 — now arrived at by solving an equation, not handed to you as a formula to memorize.

What the sign of log-odds tells you

This is genuinely useful, quick mental math you can do on any log-odds value.

log-odds signequivalent statementmeaning
positiveP > 1−P  (i.e. P > 0.5)class 1 is more likely
zeroP = 1−P  (P = 0.5)a genuine coin flip
negative1−P > P  (P < 0.5)class 0 is more likely
Worked checks: log(0.6/0.4) = log(1.5) > 0 ✓ (P=0.6>0.5). log(0.4/0.6) = log(0.667) < 0 ✓ (P=0.4<0.5). log(3/2) > 0 while log(2/3) < 0 — flipping the ratio flips the sign, exactly as expected. And log(1) = 0 exactly at P=0.5, our familiar decision boundary.
Beyond two classes

Multiclass Classification — One vs. Rest

We've dealt with 2 classes so far (0/1). What if there are more than two? Suppose we're classifying fruit images into three classes: Orange, Apple, Grape.

The trick: build one binary model per class

Instead of one 3-way model, train 3 separate ordinary logistic regressions, each asking a simple yes/no question:

modelquestion it answers
M₁Orange vs. Not-Orange
M₂Apple vs. Not-Apple
M₃Grape vs. Not-Grape

What the training data looks like for each model

Every row gets a different 0/1 label depending on which model is looking at it:

rowtrue classM₁ label (Orange?)M₂ label (Apple?)M₃ label (Grape?)
D₁Orange100
D₂Apple010
D₃Grape001
D₄Grape001
D₅Apple010
D₆Apple010

How do we predict now? Argmax across the three models

A new test point gets passed through all three trained models, each returning its own probability. Whichever model comes back most confident wins — we simply take the largest of the three:

predicted class = argmax( ŷ_orange, ŷ_apple, ŷ_grape )
modeloutput probability
M₁ (Orange)0.92
M₂ (Apple)0.13
M₃ (Grape)0.01
argmax(0.92, 0.13, 0.01) = 0.92 → predicted class = Orange. Notice the three probabilities don't need to sum to 1 (each model was trained completely independently, asking its own yes/no question) — we're just comparing raw confidence, not a proper joint probability distribution.
⭐ Optional — quiz section

Test yourself

Two quick checks from class — try them before revealing the answer.

Q1How do outliers affect the classification boundary in logistic regression?

A) Outliers shift the boundary closer to the outlier values  B) No effect  C) Outliers widen the gap between boundaries  D) Outliers make the boundary more sensitive to minor changes

Reveal answer
A — outliers shift the boundary closer to the outlier values. This follows directly from the Case 2 analysis above: a misclassified outlier contributes an enormous loss (≈4.31 vs ≈0.0003 for a correctly-classified one). Since training minimizes total loss, the fit is pulled hard toward reducing that one dominant term — bending the boundary toward the outlier, even at the expense of fitting the rest of the data slightly worse.
Q2Choosing the best λ. Testing λ=[1, 0.1, 0.01] gives:
Train accuracy: [0.90, 0.98, 0.93]    Test accuracy: [0.78, 0.99, 0.97]
Which λ is best?
Reveal answer
λ=0.1 is best. Reading each candidate:
• λ=1 (strongest penalty): train=0.90, test=0.78 — a large gap, and neither number is great. Too much regularization is squeezing out real signal.
• λ=0.1: train=0.98, test=0.99 — both high, test even edges out train, essentially no concerning gap. This is the balanced sweet spot.
• λ=0.01 (weakest penalty): train=0.93, test=0.97 — decent, but this is the direction where overfitting risk grows as you keep decreasing λ further.
General rule used here: don't just chase the highest single number — look for the smallest train-test gap combined with strong test performance.

Deriving the weight update (gradient descent)

If you want to see exactly how the weights get found — not required to use or trust the model, just here for the curious. Slower this time, one algebra step per line.

Setup — the chain rule plan

For one sample: Lᵢ = −[yᵢ·log(pᵢ) + (1−yᵢ)·log(1−pᵢ)],   pᵢ=σ(zᵢ),   zᵢ=w·xᵢ. We need ∂Lᵢ/∂w. It isn't direct — w affects z, z affects p, p affects L. Three linked pieces:

∂Lᵢ/∂w  =  (∂Lᵢ/∂pᵢ)  ×  (∂pᵢ/∂zᵢ)  ×  (∂zᵢ/∂w)

Piece 1 — ∂Lᵢ/∂pᵢ

Ordinary calculus: the derivative of −log(x) is −1/x. Apply it to each term of Lᵢ separately:

1 d/dpᵢ [ −yᵢ·log(pᵢ) ]  =  −yᵢ/pᵢ
2 d/dpᵢ [ −(1−yᵢ)·log(1−pᵢ) ]  =  +(1−yᵢ)/(1−pᵢ)  (chain rule: derivative of log(1−p) is −1/(1−p), and the outer minus flips it back to plus)
3 — add them ∂Lᵢ/∂pᵢ  =  −yᵢ/pᵢ  +  (1−yᵢ)/(1−pᵢ)

Piece 2 — ∂pᵢ/∂zᵢ, the sigmoid's own derivative

Derive it once, via the quotient rule, on σ(z) = 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ). Let u=1, v=1+e⁻ᶻ, so σ=u/v and (u/v)′=(u′v−uv′)/v².

1 u′=0, and v′ = d/dz[1+e⁻ᶻ] = −e⁻ᶻ
2 σ′(z)  =  [0·v − 1·(−e⁻ᶻ)] / v²  =  e⁻ᶻ / (1+e⁻ᶻ)²
3 Split this into two separate fractions multiplied together:   e⁻ᶻ/(1+e⁻ᶻ)²  =  [1/(1+e⁻ᶻ)]  ×  [e⁻ᶻ/(1+e⁻ᶻ)]
4 The first bracket is just σ(z). The second bracket equals 1−σ(z) — check: 1−σ(z) = 1 − 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ) = e⁻ᶻ/(1+e⁻ᶻ) ✓
∂pᵢ/∂zᵢ = σ′(zᵢ) = pᵢ(1−pᵢ)

Piece 3 — multiply Piece 1 × Piece 2, and watch it collapse

1 — write the product ∂Lᵢ/∂zᵢ  =  [ −yᵢ/pᵢ + (1−yᵢ)/(1−pᵢ) ]  ×  pᵢ(1−pᵢ)
2 — distribute the first term −yᵢ/pᵢ  ×  pᵢ(1−pᵢ)  =  −yᵢ(1−pᵢ)
3 — distribute the second term (1−yᵢ)/(1−pᵢ)  ×  pᵢ(1−pᵢ)  =  (1−yᵢ)pᵢ
4 — add them together ∂Lᵢ/∂zᵢ  =  −yᵢ(1−pᵢ) + (1−yᵢ)pᵢ
5 — expand both products =   −yᵢ + yᵢpᵢ + pᵢ − yᵢpᵢ
6 — the yᵢpᵢ terms cancel =   −yᵢ + pᵢ
∂Lᵢ/∂zᵢ  =  pᵢ − yᵢ
Everything — the −y/p, the (1−y)/(1−p), the whole sigmoid derivative p(1−p) — collapses into one clean subtraction: predicted minus actual. Same residual quantity that drives linear regression's gradient.

Final piece, and the update rule

1 ∂zᵢ/∂w = xᵢ  (trivial — zᵢ=w·xᵢ is linear in w)
2 — multiply through ∂Lᵢ/∂w  =  (∂Lᵢ/∂zᵢ) × (∂zᵢ/∂w)  =  (pᵢ−yᵢ)·xᵢ
3 — average over all n samples, step downhill w := w − η · (1/n) Σᵢ (pᵢ−yᵢ)·xᵢ
w := w − η · (1/n) Σᵢ (pᵢ−yᵢ)·xᵢ
Identical shape to linear regression's update rule — "learning rate × average of (residual × feature)." Only p vs ŷ differs. And because Log Loss is convex (unlike MSE+sigmoid), this update reliably converges to the same best answer regardless of starting point.
