Where we left off

Recap — Class 11 in one paragraph

KNN predicts by finding the K closest training points to a query and averaging (regression) or voting (classification). No training step exists in the usual sense — .fit() just stores the data. Small K overfits to individual noisy points; large K collapses to the global mean/majority. Weighted KNN (weight = 1/distance) makes plain KNN more resistant to noise, but not immune. Today we get hands-on: how expensive is a single prediction, does our code actually scale correctly, does dimensionality reduction help or hurt KNN, how do we pick K properly, what happens with 3+ classes, and how do we handle the elephant in the room — imbalanced data.

Complexity

Time Complexity of a Single KNN Query

Let's build this up step by step, exactly as it happens inside .predict() for one query point.

Step 1 — Compute distance to every training point

1 Say we have n training points, each with d features.
2 Computing ONE distance (e.g. Euclidean) between the query and one training point costs O(d) — subtract, square, sum across all d features.
3 We must do this against every one of the n training points.
Total cost of Step 1 = n × O(d) = O(n·d)

Step 2 — Sort (or partially sort) to find the K nearest

We don't need a full sort of all n distances — we only need the K smallest. A full sort costs O(n log n); a partial selection (like a heap of size K) costs only O(n log K).

Total cost of Step 2 = O(n·log K)

Step 3 — Combine (mean or majority vote)

Averaging or counting labels across K neighbors is just O(K) — negligible next to the other two steps.

Total time for ONE query = O(n·d) + O(n·log K) + O(K)  ≈  O(n·d) for realistic K, d
The dominant term is n·d — and it scales with EVERY new prediction. Predict on 1,000 new customers against our churn training set (n=1,999 rows, d=5 features) → 1,000 × 1,999 × 5 ≈ 10 million distance computations, just for that one batch. This is the direct, concrete reason KNN is called a "lazy learner" — all the work that other models do once at training time, KNN repeats fully at every single prediction.
Contrast with Logistic Regression: once trained, predicting one point costs O(d) — just a dot product with the learned weight vector. KNN's O(n·d) vs Logistic Regression's O(d) is the entire reason KNN struggles in production when n grows into the millions.

Dimensionality Reduction

PCA, in one paragraph

Principal Component Analysis is a dimensionality reduction technique. In plain terms: it takes your original features (here, 7 spend/region columns) and builds a small number of brand-new "super-features" (called components — f1, f2, ...) that are just weighted combinations of the originals, chosen so the FIRST new feature captures as much of the data's spread as mathematically possible, the second captures as much of what's left, and so on. We keep only the first couple of components and drop the rest — compressing 7 numbers per customer down to 2, while trying to lose as little information as possible.

We're only using PCA here as a tool to get 2D data we can plot and feed into KNN. The full math behind it (eigenvectors, eigenvalues, how the components are actually computed) is its own topic — we'll cover that properly, in depth, in the Unsupervised Learning module. For today, treat PCA as: "a technique that compresses correlated features into fewer, information-preserving axes."

What "Explained Variance" Actually Means

"Explained variance ratio" is simply: what fraction of the data's total spread does this one new component capture, on its own? Higher = that component alone tells you more about how customers actually differ from each other.

What we actually computed on our data

86.5% is a good sign — it means going from 7 features down to just 2 (f1, f2) still keeps most of what actually distinguishes one customer from another. That's the entire justification for reducing to 2D here: we're not losing much, and now we can plot it.

Does PCA Even Make Sense Before KNN?

The question worth asking out loud: KNN is entirely about distances. If PCA changes the coordinates, does "closer in the reduced 2D space" still mean "closer in the original 7D space"? If not, why are we doing this at all?

The honest answer: approximately yes, and that's the whole point

PCA doesn't invent new information — every component is a combination of the real features, chosen specifically to preserve as much of the true spread (and therefore true relative distances) as possible in the components it keeps. Two customers who are genuinely similar across all 7 original features will almost always still look similar in the top 2 PCA components, because those 2 dimensions were chosen to capture most of where the real variation lives. It's not an exact guarantee — a little distance information always lives in the components we dropped — but with 86.5% variance retained here, it's a close approximation, not a coincidence.

So what does doing this actually signify / give us?

BenefitWhy it matters for KNN specifically
Fewer dimensions to compute distance overDirectly cuts the "d" in our O(n·d) complexity from Step 1
Mitigates the curse of dimensionalityFewer, more informative dimensions keep "nearest" meaningfully different from "farthest"
You can actually plot and visually sanity-check the data2D scatter plots are something a human can look at and reason about directly

Should you always do PCA before KNN? Not automatically — only when it genuinely helps (a real curse-of-dimensionality problem, or a real need to visualize). Check the explained variance you're keeping, and compare KNN's actual validation performance with and without PCA before trusting it on a new dataset.

Multiclass KNN

Odd vs Even K, and What Happens on a Tie

The rule that only half-protects you

Binary classification, odd K: a tie is mathematically impossible (you can't split an odd number into two equal halves). 3+ classes: odd K does NOT protect you — a 6-way vote can still split 2-2-2 across three classes, or any other even split across 3+ groups.

A real, extreme 3-way tie — and sklearn gets it wrong

sklearn breaks multiclass ties by picking the smallest numeric label among the tied classes — here, Class 0 wins purely because "0" is the smallest number, despite Class 1 having, by far, the two genuinely closest neighbors (distance 0.5 vs 6.7+). This is not a rare corner case — any time you have 3+ classes, ties like this can and do happen.

Two Custom Tie-Breaking Implementations

Custom Implementation 1 — Nearest-neighbor tiebreak

Rule: when the vote is tied, fall back to whichever tied class contains the single closest neighbor overall.

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

def knn_predict_nearest_tiebreak(X_train, y_train, x_query, k=6):
    """Custom Implementation 1: Nearest-Neighbor Tiebreak"""
    distances = np.linalg.norm(X_train - x_query, axis=1)
    idx = np.argsort(distances)[:k]
    neighbor_labels = y_train[idx]
    neighbor_dists  = distances[idx]

    counts = Counter(neighbor_labels)
    max_votes = max(counts.values())
    tied_classes = [c for c, v in counts.items() if v == max_votes]

    if len(tied_classes) == 1:
        return tied_classes[0]

    # Tie -> fall back to the single closest neighbor's label
    nearest_label = neighbor_labels[np.argmin(neighbor_dists)]
    return nearest_label
On our tied example → predicts Class 1 (correct — verified by running this exact function)

Custom Implementation 2 — Weighted-vote tiebreak

Rule: when the vote is tied, compute the sum of 1/distance only among the tied classes, and let the class with the highest total weight win.

def knn_predict_weighted_tiebreak(X_train, y_train, x_query, k=6):
    """Custom Implementation 2: Weighted-Vote Tiebreak"""
    distances = np.linalg.norm(X_train - x_query, axis=1)
    idx = np.argsort(distances)[:k]
    neighbor_labels = y_train[idx]
    neighbor_dists  = distances[idx]

    counts = Counter(neighbor_labels)
    max_votes = max(counts.values())
    tied_classes = [c for c, v in counts.items() if v == max_votes]

    if len(tied_classes) == 1:
        return tied_classes[0]

    # Tie -> sum(1/distance) among only the tied classes
    weight_sums = {}
    for c in tied_classes:
        mask = neighbor_labels == c
        weight_sums[c] = sum(1/d if d > 0 else 1e12 for d in neighbor_dists[mask])
    return max(weight_sums, key=weight_sums.get)

Class 01/6.727 + 1/7.433 = 0.283

Note Class 0 and Class 2 remain exactly tied with each other here (0.283 = 0.283) — a purely symmetric edge case. In practice, exact numeric ties this deep are vanishingly rare; what matters is that the OBVIOUS winner (Class 1) is no longer lost to arbitrary label ordering.

Class Imbalance

The Problem Statement

Our churn dataset: 2,850 customers did not churn, 483 did — churn is only 14.5% of the data. Any model can score 85.5% accuracy by just always predicting "No Churn" and never learning anything real. Business cost is asymmetric too: missing an actual churner (false negative) is usually far more expensive than a false alarm (false positive) — you lose the customer either way if you don't act.

Verified directly: an unweighted Logistic Regression on this data gets churn-class F1 of only 31.4% — it is quietly failing at the one thing the business actually needs, while still looking "accurate" overall.

Two Approaches: Cost-Sensitive vs Sampling

Cost-sensitive learning

Keep the data exactly as it is. Instead, change the loss function so mistakes on the minority class are penalized more heavily than mistakes on the majority class. The model itself is told "getting this class wrong costs more."

Sampling

Keep the loss function exactly as it is. Instead, change the data itself — add more minority examples (oversampling/SMOTE) or remove majority examples (undersampling) until the classes are balanced before training even starts.

Neither is universally "better" — cost-sensitive learning costs nothing extra computationally and works for any model with a class_weight-style parameter; sampling works with ANY model (including KNN, which has no such parameter) but changes your actual training set.

Logistic Regression: class_weight, Set Two Ways

Way 1 — manually pick the numbers yourself

LogisticRegression(class_weight={{0: 1, 1: 6}})

You directly tell sklearn: every mistake on class 1 (churn) counts 6× as much as a mistake on class 0. Where does "6" come from? From the actual class ratio in our training data:

1718 non-churn / 281 churn ≈ 6.11  →  rounded to 6 for the manual setting

Way 2 — let sklearn compute it for you

LogisticRegression(class_weight='balanced')

sklearn automatically computes the exact inverse-frequency weight per class from whatever data you fit on — no manual arithmetic, and it recalculates correctly if your class balance ever changes.

class_weight='balanced' — The Exact Formula

wc = ntotal / (nclasses × nc)

Binary case — our exact training data (1718 non-churn, 281 churn, n=1999)

How the Loss Function Actually Changes

Ordinary binary cross-entropy (every row counted equally)

L = −(1/n) Σ [ yi log(pi) + (1−yi) log(1−pi) ]

Weighted version

Lweighted = −(1/n) Σ wyi [ yi log(pi) + (1−yi) log(1−pi) ]

Every churn row's error now gets multiplied by 3.5569 before being added to the total loss; every non-churn row's error gets multiplied by 0.5818. The optimizer is mathematically forced to care more about getting churners right, because leaving a churner misclassified now costs the loss function much more.

Real before/after on our churn validation set

Churn-class F1Recall (catching real churners)Precision
No weighting31.4%21.8%56.4%
class_weight={{0:1,1:6}}46.6%74.3%33.9%
Recall more than triples (21.8% → 74.3%) — the model now catches three times as many real churners. The cost: precision drops (56.4% → 33.9%), meaning more false alarms. This is the fundamental trade-off of every imbalance fix: fewer missed churners always comes at the cost of more false alarms — which trade-off is worth it is a business decision, not a modeling one.
Sampling Techniques

Undersampling — Mechanism and Math

The mechanism

Randomly remove majority-class rows, without replacement, until both classes have the same count as the minority class.

1718 non-churners → randomly cut down to 281, to match the 281 real churners.
1718 − 281 = 1437 real, genuine rows permanently discarded
ProsCons
Fast (much smaller training set); no synthetic-data assumptionsThrows away 1437 real rows — a huge amount of genuine information, especially risky if the majority class had rare-but-important sub-patterns
Result — Train F1: 77.3%   Test F1: 49.2%
The large train/test F1 gap (77% → 49%) is itself informative — with fewer than 300 majority rows left, the model is prone to overfitting to whichever majority examples happened to survive the random cut. Rule of thumb: only undersample when n is genuinely huge (hundreds of thousands to billions of rows) — here, with only 1999 training rows to begin with, throwing away 1437 of them is a lot to lose.

Oversampling — Mechanism and Math

The mechanism

Randomly duplicate minority-class rows, WITH replacement, until the minority count matches the majority count.

281 real churners → copied (with repeats) up to 1718, to match the 1718 non-churners.
Verified directly: 83.6% of the "new" minority rows are exact duplicates of an existing row
ProsCons
Simple, no rows discarded, works with any modelAdds zero new information — the model can end up seeing the same handful of churners repeated 6× each, risking memorization of those specific rows
Result — Train F1: 76.6%   Test F1: 50.7%

SMOTE — Mechanism, Math, and a Real Synthetic Point

The mechanism, step by step

1 Pick a real minority-class point (a real churner).
2 Find its K nearest neighbors, searching ONLY among other minority-class points (K=5 by default).
3 Pick one of those neighbors at random.
4 Generate a synthetic point somewhere on the straight line between the two real points:
new_point = point + random(0,1) × (neighbor − point)
5 Repeat until the minority class reaches the majority count.
