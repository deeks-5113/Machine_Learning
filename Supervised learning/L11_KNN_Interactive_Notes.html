Where we left off

Recap — what logistic regression actually draws

Every model we've built so far — Linear Regression, Logistic Regression — works the same way underneath: find the best weights, and draw a straight line (2D), plane (3D), or hyperplane (higher-D) that either fits the trend or separates the classes.

Logistic regression's whole job, in one sentence

Find one straight boundary such that — as much as possible — every point of Class A lands on one side, and every point of Class B lands on the other.

One straight line. Every Class A point sits above it, every Class B point sits below it — a clean, verified separation.
This works beautifully — as long as such a line actually exists. That's the entire assumption logistic regression is built on: linear separability.

But what if there's no straight line?

What happens when Class A and Class B are arranged so that NO straight line, anywhere, in any direction, can separate them?

The classic pattern no straight line can ever cut: one class surrounds the other

Class A sits in a tight cluster in the middle. Class B forms a ring all the way around it. Draw any straight line you like — it will always cut through both classes.
No matter how you rotate or shift a straight line, it always has Class B on BOTH sides of it (since B surrounds A on every side) — so it can never isolate Class A cleanly. Logistic regression's core assumption has broken down completely here.
The different approach: instead of learning one global rule that applies everywhere, just look at what's physically closest to the point you're asking about, right now, locally. This is the entire idea behind KNN.
The intuition

House Price — just ask your neighbors

You've just moved to a new locality and want to guess what your house is roughly worth. You don't build a formula. You do the obvious human thing: you look at a few houses nearby that are similar in size and distance from the city, check what they sold for, and average it out in your head. "The house next door sold for 50L, the one two streets down sold for 52L... mine's probably around there too."

That instinct IS the algorithm

No line. No equation to fit. No coefficients to learn. Just: find similar examples, look at what happened to them, use that as your answer. This is exactly what K-Nearest-Neighbors does — formalized into four precise steps we'll build one at a time today.

Euclidean Distance — the straight-line distance

The direct, shortest-possible distance between two points — exactly the geometry you learned in school (draw a ruler-straight line from A to B and measure it). Sometimes called "as the crow flies" since a bird can fly straight over obstacles, unlike a person on the ground.

d(A,B) = √( (sizeA−sizeB)² + (distA−distB)² )

Manhattan Distance — distance along a grid, no diagonals allowed

Add up the horizontal gap and the vertical gap separately — you're not allowed to cut diagonally between them. Named after Manhattan's street grid, where a taxi can only drive along blocks (north-south, then east-west), never straight through a building.

d(A,B) = |sizeA−sizeB| + |distA−distB|

From neighbors to a prediction

Sort it, take the top K

Once every training point has a distance attached, the next two steps are almost mechanical.

1 Sort all 8 houses by their Euclidean distance, smallest first (already done in the table above): H1, H7, H2, H3, H8, H5, H4, H6.
2 Pick K — say K=3. Take just the first 3 rows of that sorted list: H1, H7, H2. Everything else (H3, H8, H5, H4, H6) is completely ignored, no matter how "similar" they might otherwise seem.

Visually: the K=3 neighborhood around our query

Dashed lines = the 3 nearest houses (K=3). Everything else is simply not consulted.

From neighbors to an answer — mean vs majority

Regression — average the neighbors' values

predicted price = mean( priceH1, priceH7, priceH2 )
= (50 + 52 + 55) / 3 = 52.33
Our query house is predicted at Rs 52.33 Lakh — simply the average of its 3 nearest neighbors' actual sale prices.

Classification — majority vote among the neighbors

If instead we were predicting a category (say, "Premium" vs "Budget" house), we'd just count labels among the K nearest neighbors and go with whichever appears most.

3 neighbors vote: 2 say "Budget", 1 says "Premium" → predicted = Budget
This is literally a vote — whichever label has the most supporters among the K neighbors wins. Ties (e.g. 2 vs 2 with K=4) are usually broken by picking the label of the single closest point, or by reducing K by one.

"Training" a KNN model is almost a non-event — .fit() just stores X_train and y_train in memory (optionally building a fast lookup structure like a KD-Tree). All the real computation happens later, at .predict() time, against the stored training data.

Choosing K — sklearn's default, and your job as the modeler

sklearn's default

KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)   |   KNeighborsRegressor(n_neighbors=5)

Both default to K=5 if you don't specify it. That's a reasonable generic starting point — but it is NOT guaranteed to be the best K for your specific dataset.

K is a hyperparameter — YOU must tune it

Exactly like alpha in Ridge/Lasso: K is not learned from the training data automatically. You try several values of K, measure performance on your validation set for each, and keep whichever K generalizes best.

1 Fit KNN on the training set with K=1, check validation accuracy/error.
2 Repeat for K=3, 5, 7, 9, ... up to some reasonable range.
3 Plot validation performance vs K. Pick the K at the best point (we'll see exactly what this looks like shortly).

Underfitting — K = all the data

Push K all the way up to K = n (every single training point becomes a "neighbor," always). Watch what happens.

Regression: K=8 (all houses)

predicted price = mean(all 8 prices) = 53.12
Every possible query house — big or small, near the city or far — gets predicted at exactly Rs 53.12L. The location of the query stops mattering entirely. The model has stopped "looking locally" and just repeats the same global average forever.

Classification: K=9 (all customers)

Global vote: 4 Yes vs 5 No → majority = No
Every possible query customer gets predicted "No" — regardless of their actual age or spending pattern — simply because No happens to be the overall majority class in the training data.
This is underfitting, textbook-clean: the model has become too rigid to react to the specific input at all. It has collapsed to "always predict the global average / global majority" — exactly as flat and unhelpful as a straight horizontal line would be for a regression problem with real curvature.

Overfitting — K = 1, explained properly

The puzzle worth sitting with: KNN doesn't "train" anything — there's no gradient descent, no weights, no loss function being minimized. So when K=1 "overfits," what is actually overfitting? There's no model to have memorized anything... or is there?

The resolution: "training" in KNN means something different, not nothing

Overfitting, defined generally (not just for weight-based models), means: the prediction is overly sensitive to the specific noise/idiosyncrasies of individual training points, rather than reflecting the true general pattern. KNN with K=1 fits this definition exactly — just mechanically instead of via gradients.

With K=1, a query's prediction is decided by exactly one single training point — whichever happens to be closest. There is zero opportunity for other nearby points to "outvote" or "average out" that one point's noise, mislabeling, or measurement error. One weird data point can hijack the answer for an entire neighborhood around it.

Watch it happen — our Buy / No-Buy customers, with one noisy label

C72722Yes2.24

Query customer (blue diamond). C9 (ringed in black) is a mislabeled "No" sitting in the middle of a "Yes" neighborhood.

K=1 blindly trusted a single noisy point with zero skepticism. The moment we let K=3 neighbors vote, the two genuine "Yes" neighbors outvoted the one noisy "No" — the noise got smoothed out. That smoothing-out effect is precisely what K=1 is missing, and precisely why it overfits.

Why K=1's "training accuracy" is deceptively perfect

If you naively check how well a K=1 model "fits" the training data itself (each training point's nearest neighbor, including itself, at distance 0), you get 100% training accuracy every single time, no matter how messy the data is — every point's closest match is trivially itself. This looks like a perfect fit. It is NOT evidence of a good model — it's evidence of nothing more than memorization. The real test is always unseen/validation data, where K=1's hypersensitivity to single points shows up as poor, jagged, unstable predictions.

Finding the best value of K

Exactly like tuning alpha in Ridge/Lasso: sweep K across a range, measure error on the validation set at each value, and look for the sweet spot between the two failure modes we just saw.

Training error keeps falling as K shrinks toward 1 (memorization). Validation error is U-shaped — that's where the real answer lives.
Small K (left side) → low training error, high validation error → overfitting. Large K (right side) → both errors high and close together → underfitting. The best K sits at the bottom of the validation curve — found empirically, never guessed.
Weighted KNN

The idea — and why we need it

Plain KNN treats every one of the K neighbors as equally important — the closest neighbor and the K-th closest neighbor get an identical vote/weight. That feels wrong: shouldn't a neighbor who is almost on top of your query matter more than one who is barely inside the K cutoff?

The fix: weight each neighbor by how close it is

weight = 1 / distance   (closer → bigger weight, farther → smaller weight)
As distance shrinks toward 0, weight shoots up. As distance grows, weight fades toward 0 — but never quite reaches it.
Instead of a hard cutoff ("you're one of the K nearest, so you get one full vote"), every neighbor contributes some influence, scaled smoothly by how close it actually is. This is a strictly richer signal than plain KNN's "all K neighbors count equally, everyone else counts zero."

Does Weighted KNN fix K=1 overfitting? The nuanced answer

Your exact question: "if a closer point gets MORE weight, won't a very close noisy/mislabeled point get even MORE precedence, not less? Doesn't that make things worse?" Let's actually check, with real numbers.

First: at K=1, weighting changes nothing at all

With K=1 there is only ONE neighbor being considered — giving it "weight 1/distance" instead of "weight 1" doesn't change who wins, because there's no second vote to compare against. Weighted KNN does NOT fix K=1 overfitting — if your single nearest neighbor is noisy, weighting is irrelevant, you still get fooled every time.

Where weighting actually helps: K ≥ 3, with a MODERATELY close noisy point

Back to our Buy/No-Buy example. At K=3, the neighbors are C9 (noisy, No, distance 2.00), C7 (Yes, distance 2.24), C1 (Yes, distance 3.00):

CustomerLabelDistanceWeight = 1/distance
C9No2.000.500
C7Yes2.240.447
C1Yes3.000.333
Yes total weight = 0.447+0.333 = 0.781   vs   No total weight = 0.500 → predicted = Yes (correct!)
Even though C9 is the single closest point (so it gets the single highest weight), the COMBINED weight of the two genuine "Yes" neighbors still wins. Weighted KNN resisted the noise here.

But your instinct is right — think of it as a tug-of-war

Picture a tug-of-war rope. On one side stand C7 and C1 together, pulling with a combined strength of 0.781 — that number never changes, no matter what C9 does. On the other side stands C9 alone. Right now C9 is 2.00 units away, so it only pulls with strength 0.500 — not enough to win. But watch what happens as we drag C9 closer and closer to the query, making it pull harder each time:

Top bar in each row = the combined "Yes team" pull (always 0.781). Bottom bar = C9 pulling alone, growing as it gets closer. The moment C9's own bar passes the Yes team's bar, C9 wins the tug-of-war by itself.

Reading the five rows top to bottom: at distance 2.00, 1.50, and 1.28, C9's solo pull still loses to the Yes team's combined pull — weighted KNN correctly says "Yes." But once C9 is dragged to distance 1.00 (and closer), its solo pull becomes strong enough to beat the Yes team's combined pull all by itself — weighted KNN flips to the wrong answer, "No."

Your intuition was exactly correct. Weighted KNN is more ROBUST than plain KNN — it takes a much closer noisy point to fool it, since that one point now has to out-PULL the combined strength of every other neighbor, not just out-RANK them. But it is NOT immune. An extremely close noisy point can still win the tug-of-war alone.
The precise, general statement: plain KNN (K=1) is fooled by ANY nearest noisy point, no matter its exact distance — it only cares about rank ("who's closest"), not magnitude ("how much closer"). Weighted KNN is fooled only when a noisy point is disproportionately closer than everything else combined — a much narrower, harder-to-trigger failure condition. Weighted KNN raises the bar for being fooled; it doesn't remove the possibility entirely.

Weighted KNN for Regression — formula and worked example

y_pred = Σ( wi × yi ) / Σ( wi )     where wi = 1 / distancei

Instead of a plain average, this is a weighted average — every neighbor's price is multiplied by its own weight before summing, then divided by the total weight (so everything stays on the correct scale).

K=3 worked example

HousePriceDistanceWeight (1/d)Weight x Price
H1501.001.00050.00
H7521.120.89446.51
H2552.000.50027.50
y_pred = (1.000x50 + 0.894x52 + 0.500x55) / (1.000 + 0.894 + 0.500)
= 124.01 / 2.394 = 51.79
Plain KNN (K=3) predicted 52.33. Weighted KNN predicts 51.79 — slightly lower, because H1 (the closest, cheapest of the three at Rs50L) now pulls harder than the plain average allowed, while H2 (farthest of the three, most expensive at Rs55L) has its influence reduced.

That's the entire point of weighting: far-away neighbors are still consulted, but they can no longer drag the answer as hard as very close ones can. Weighting makes the prediction much less sensitive to exactly which K you pick.

Weighted KNN for Classification — formula and worked example

predicted class = argmaxc Σ( wi × [yi = c] )     sum the weights of neighbors belonging to each class, pick the class with the bigger total

Instead of "count votes," it's "sum weights per class." We already computed this in the previous section — here it is laid out as the general recipe:

ClassNeighbors contributingSum of their weights
YesC7 (0.447) + C1 (0.333)0.781
NoC9 (0.500)0.500
argmax(0.781, 0.500) = Yes

How this differs from plain regression / plain classification

Plain KNNWeighted KNN
Regression combine stepsimple mean of K pricesweighted average, weight = 1/distance
Classification combine stepcount of labels ("votes"), each worth 1sum of weights per label, each neighbor's "vote" worth 1/distance
Sensitivity to far-but-included neighborsfull influence, same as close onesheavily discounted, but never fully zero
Sensitivity to exact K choicehigh — adding one more neighbor changes the average/vote a lotlower — newly added far neighbors barely move the answer

Is K in Weighted KNN chosen the same way?

Yes — K is still a hyperparameter you tune via the validation set, exactly as before (sklearn default is still K=5 for KNeighborsClassifier(weights='distance') / KNeighborsRegressor(weights='distance')). Nothing about the tuning PROCESS changes.

What DOES change: because far neighbors are automatically down-weighted, weighted KNN's validation curve tends to be flatter and more forgiving around the best K — picking K=7 instead of the true best K=5 hurts you less under weighting than under plain voting, since those extra 2 neighbors barely get a say anyway. It's still worth tuning properly, but weighted KNN is more forgiving of a slightly-off K choice.
Things that break KNN

Outliers in KNN — what, how to detect, what to do

What is an outlier, in KNN's specific context?

Any training point that sits far from its own class's natural cluster — either because of a genuine one-off unusual case, a data entry error, or a mislabeling. C9 from our classification example (a "No" customer sitting deep inside the "Yes" neighborhood) is exactly this kind of outlier.

Why outliers hurt KNN specifically (more than some other models)

KNN has no global fitting step that could "average out" or "smooth over" one bad point using the whole dataset's pattern (the way a regression line is shaped by every point at once). An outlier just sits there in feature space, fully able to become the nearest neighbor for any future query that happens to land near it — poisoning predictions locally, indefinitely, with no correction mechanism.

How to detect outliers before they cause damage

1 Visualize (2D/3D scatter, or pairplots) — a point sitting alone, far from same-class peers, is visually obvious in low dimensions.
2 Distance-based check: for every training point, compute the distance to its own nearest neighbor(s). Points whose nearest neighbor is unusually far away are structurally isolated — classic outlier signature.
3 Label-disagreement check (for classification): for every point, look at its own K nearest OTHER points — if the vast majority disagree with its label (like C9's neighbors all being "Yes"), that point is very likely mislabeled noise.
4 Statistical checks on individual numeric features: z-score beyond ~3, or IQR-based fences, flag individually extreme values worth double-checking.

What to do once you've found one

SituationWhat to do
Genuine data entry errorFix it if you can verify the correct value; otherwise remove the row
Genuinely rare but valid caseKeep it, but consider Weighted KNN and/or a slightly larger K so it can't dominate alone
Likely mislabeled (like our C9)Re-verify the label with the source of truth; if wrong, fix or remove
Can't tell / not verifiableFavor a larger K and Weighted KNN — both dilute a single bad point's influence, as we proved above

Class Imbalance — where plain KNN fails

KNN's vote is a raw head-count. If one class vastly outnumbers another in the training data, that imbalance leaks directly into every single neighborhood, everywhere in feature space — even in regions that "belong" to the minority class.

The visual failure mode — a concrete K=11 example

30 majority points vs 6 minority points. The query genuinely sits in "minority territory" — but its K=11 neighborhood (dashed circle) reaches far enough to sweep in majority points too.
K=11 nearest neighbors: 7 Majority vs 4 Minority → plain vote predicts Majority (WRONG)
Even though the query sits closest to the minority cluster, K=11 reaches out far enough to scoop up 7 majority-class points from the surrounding sea, against only 4 minority points — and because plain KNN counts every neighbor as one equal vote, those 7 majority votes numerically overwhelm the 4 closer, more relevant minority votes.

Why this specifically gets worse as K grows

Small K stays local enough to still "see" the minority cluster correctly. As K grows toward the underfitting end of the spectrum, you are (by definition) sweeping in a wider and wider neighborhood — and a wider neighborhood in imbalanced data disproportionately pulls in more majority-class points, simply because there are so many more of them everywhere. Imbalance and the underfitting end of K compound each other.

Does Weighted KNN survive class imbalance?

Partially — it helps, but it is not a fix. Same K=11 example, now weighted:

Weighted KNN gives closer points more say. Let's check if that rescues our K=11 example above, using weight = 1/distance for all 11 neighbors:

Majority total weight = 0.196   vs   Minority total weight = 0.102 → weighted KNN ALSO predicts Majority (STILL WRONG)
Weighting did not save us here. Even after discounting every majority point by its distance, there are simply enough of them, spread closely enough, that their combined weight still beats the minority cluster's combined weight. Distance-discounting reduced the gap (compare 7-vs-4 head count to 0.196-vs-0.102 weight — the ratio did shrink) but didn't flip the outcome.

The general rule this demonstrates

Weighted KNN fixes imbalance problems that are really about DISTANCE (a few very-close relevant neighbors buried under many farther irrelevant ones, where those far ones are few enough that discounting them is sufficient). It does NOT fix imbalance problems that are really about RAW COUNT (the majority class is so numerous that even individually-discounted, their sheer numbers add up to more combined weight than the whole minority cluster can muster).

Sheer count can still out-muscle proximity: a hundred distant majority points, each contributing a small weight, can still out-total two or three close minority points. For that, you need to change the DATA's balance itself, not the algorithm — which is exactly where we're headed next.
Looking ahead

Oversampling & Undersampling — a quick introduction

If plain and weighted KNN both struggle when one class is simply too rare, the fix is to change the training data's balance itself before KNN ever sees it. Two opposite ways to do that:

Oversampling — add more minority examples

Duplicate (or synthetically generate) minority-class points until the two classes are closer in count. More minority points nearby means minority votes/weights can finally compete on equal footing.

Undersampling — remove some majority examples

Randomly drop majority-class points until the counts balance out. Fewer majority points means fewer of them can sweep into a query's neighborhood and swamp the vote.

Visualizing the class-count shift

Oversampling grows the minority bar to match. Undersampling shrinks the majority bar to match. (*synthetic/duplicated points)
Simple duplication for oversampling has a real weakness — it just repeats the exact same points, adding no new information, and can make KNN even MORE overconfident about those specific spots. That weakness is exactly what next class's technique, SMOTE, is built to fix — by generating new, synthetic minority points along the lines between real ones, instead of just copying.
