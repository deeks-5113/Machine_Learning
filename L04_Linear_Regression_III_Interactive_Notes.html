The reframe before the five

Why assumptions matter — the race-car analogy

"A Bugatti can hit 400 km/h — but only under specific conditions: the right track, tires, weather. Take those away, and the car doesn't stop working. It just won't reliably hit that number anymore."
Linear Regression is the Bugatti. The 5 assumptions are the conditions. Break one, and the model still fits, still predicts — it just stops being trustworthy in a specific, predictable way. Data/model "satisfies the assumptions" = the conditions under which the top speed is real.

Assumption 1

Linearity

What it says

The output y is a straight-line (or flat-plane) function of the input(s) — y changes at a constant rate as x changes, everywhere.

Single feature vs. multiple features

y = w₁x₁ + w₀ ← one feature: a straight line, y=mx+c
y = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + ... + w₀ ← many features: a flat plane / hyperplane

Why we still call both "linear": the model is a weighted sum of the inputs — each x multiplied by a fixed weight, then added. "Linear" describes that mathematical form, not literally "draws one line" once you're past one feature.

Examples — linear vs. non-linear

linear — constant rate everywhere
non-linear — car speed vs. accelerator pressure (diminishing returns)

How to check it yourself

Single feature: plot y vs. x — do points hug a straight line?
Multiple features: can't visualize directly — plot residuals vs. fitted values instead. Random scatter around 0 = healthy. Any curve/U-shape = linearity is broken, and there's a systematic pattern the model is missing.

fix: add polynomial terms (x², x³ — see the Polynomial Regression section) or transform the variable.
Assumption 2 — in full depth

No Multicollinearity

What it is

You have multiple input features — say, age of car, km driven, year of manufacture. These are supposed to be independent: knowing one shouldn't tell you much about another.

But age of car and year of manufacture basically say the same thing. A car from 2015 is 9 years old; a car from 2020 is 4 years old. Age = (current year − year of manufacture) — almost perfectly determined by one another.

Why this actually matters

It breaks feature importance — the derivation

Not a hand-wave — here's exactly what happens to the equation.

1Start with the full model:
y = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + w₃x₃ + ... + w₀
2Suppose x₃ is a linear function of x₁ (e.g. year ≈ a fixed relationship with age):
x₃ = λx₁
3Substitute x₃ back into the equation:
y = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + w₃(λx₁) + w₀ = (w₁ + w₃λ)x₁ + w₂x₂ + w₀
the coefficient that ends up attached to x₁ is really (w₁ + w₃λ) — a tangled mix of w₁ and w₃. countless different splits between w₁ and w₃ give the exact same (w₁+w₃λ), and therefore the exact same predictions. that's why coefficients become unstable while prediction barely moves.

A quick numeric illustration

Say a model reports: price = 80·age + 30·year + 55·f₃ + 45·f₄

Ranking "importance" by raw coefficient size looks reasonable — until you remember age and year are almost the same feature wearing two names. Their two numbers (80 and 30) aren't two independent effects; they're one real effect, split arbitrarily between them. You cannot trust this ranking without checking for multicollinearity first.

The real test

VIF — step by step

The procedure

For every feature xᵢ, one at a time:

1temporarily treat xᵢ as the target, and every other feature as the input
2fit a regression: xᵢ ~ (all other features)
3take the R² from that regression
4compute:
VIFi = 1 / (1 − R²ᵢ)

In code

from statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence import variance_inflation_factor vif['VIF'] = [variance_inflation_factor(X_t.values, i) for i in range(17)]

What each R² actually means for VIF

SituationWhat happens to VIF
R² ≈ 0 (feature not predictable by others)VIF ≈ 1/1 = 1 → safe, no multicollinearity
R² ≈ 0.5 (moderate prediction)VIF ≈ 1/0.5 = 2 → mild, acceptable
R² ≈ 0.9 (highly predictable by others)VIF ≈ 1/0.1 = 10 → very high, problem
R² ≈ 1.0 (perfectly predictable)VIF → ∞ → perfect multicollinearity, remove immediately
The threshold

VIF rule of thumb

VIF valueInterpretationAction
VIF < 5safe to usekeep the feature
5 ≤ VIF < 10high multicollinearitybe cautious — consider removing
VIF ≥ 10very high multicollinearityremove this feature
VIF = ∞perfect correlationremove immediately
real class example: age and year of manufacture both showed very high / near-infinite VIF. one was removed — R² barely moved (≈0.85 → still ≈0.85).
the honest conclusion — write this down:
prediction → not affected
feature importance → affected
multicollinearity is mainly a problem for trusting individual coefficients, not for overall predictive accuracy.

Assumption 3

Normal Errors

Residuals — not y itself — should form a bell curve, centered on zero.

A real residual histogram from class: a tall, tight spike around 0 — but with small extra bumps out in the tails, around ±7 to ±10. That's not a clean bell curve; it's a sign of some unusual cases the model consistently misses by a lot.

tested by: Omnibus / Prob(Omnibus), Skew, Kurtosis, Jarque-Bera (JB) / Prob(JB) — all four appear in the regression summary below.
full detail on reading each of these → Optional Read
Assumption 4

Independent Errors

One residual shouldn't predict the next — errors shouldn't "carry over" from row to row.

Most relevant for ordered/time data — weather, stock prices, day-over-day sales. If today's error being high means tomorrow's is also likely high, the model is missing a trend or pattern over time, not just being noisy.

tested by: Durbin-Watson — appears directly in the regression summary below.
full +ve / −ve autocorrelation explanation → Optional Read
Assumption 5

Homoscedasticity

The spread of residuals should stay roughly constant across all predicted values — no funnel, no fan.

Example: predicting car price. Cheap, common cars might have tightly clustered errors; rare luxury cars can swing wildly in either direction. That widening spread as price increases is heteroscedasticity.

tested by: Breusch-Pagan (or Goldfeld-Quandt) — not part of the default summary() printout; run separately via statsmodels.stats.diagnostic.het_breuschpagan.

Every statistic, explained properly

Not required for the exam-level understanding above — but here's exactly how to read every value, for whenever you need it.

F-statistic, Prob(F-statistic), AIC, BIC

F-statistic tests whether the model, taken as a whole, explains significantly more than no model at all (all coefficients = 0). Prob(F-statistic) is the p-value for that test — near 0 means yes, overwhelmingly.

AIC and BIC are model-comparison scores (built from Log-Likelihood, penalized for complexity) — lower is better, but only meaningful when comparing two candidate models on the same data, never in isolation.

coef, std err, t, P>|t|, [0.025, 0.975]

coefthe estimated weight for that feature, holding all others constant
std errhow uncertain that estimate is — smaller is more precise
tcoef ÷ std err — how many standard errors the estimate is from zero
P>|t|probability of seeing this result if the true effect were actually zero. below 0.05 = statistically significant
[0.025, 0.975]the 95% confidence interval — the range the true coefficient plausibly falls in

Omnibus, Prob(Omnibus), Skew, Kurtosis, Jarque-Bera (JB)

Omnibusa combined test of skew + kurtosis against normality — higher value = further from normal
Prob(Omnibus)the p-value for that test. below 0.05 = reject normality
Skew0 = perfectly symmetric. positive = longer right tail. negative = longer left tail
Kurtosisnormal distribution = 3. higher = heavier tails / more extreme outliers than normal (our data: 14.04, very heavy)
Jarque-Bera (JB) / Prob(JB)a second, independent normality test — same reading rule as Omnibus, often more reliable at large sample sizes
reading our real numbers: Omnibus and JB both reject normality (Prob ≈ 0). Skew (0.349) is only mildly off-center — the real damage is Kurtosis (14.04) — very heavy tails, matching the outlier bumps seen in the histogram.

Durbin-Watson — positive vs. negative autocorrelation, made clear

DW = Σ(eₜ − eₜ₋₁)² / Σeₜ²

Ranges strictly from 0 to 4.

DW → toward 0: positive autocorrelation
consecutive residuals stay the same sign — slow drift/trend
DW → toward 4: negative autocorrelation
consecutive residuals flip sign — sharp zig-zag
DW ≈ 2 → healthy, no autocorrelation either way. our real class output: 2.002 — essentially textbook-perfect independence.

Condition Number (Cond. No.)

A whole-model check for multicollinearity (unlike VIF, which checks one feature at a time). It measures how "unstable" the underlying matrix math is.

Cond. No. < 30fine, no serious concern
30 – 100moderate concern — investigate with VIF
> 100severe — real multicollinearity likely present
our real value: 10.3 — low, confirming this final model (after removing the redundant age/year pair) has no serious remaining multicollinearity.
Where a straight line isn't enough

Polynomial Regression

The equation

ŷ = w₀ + w₁x + w₂x² + w₃x³ + ...

Still linear regression — via transformed features

Rename x² as a new column z₂, x³ as z₃, and so on:

ŷ = w₀ + w₁z₁ + w₂z₂ + w₃z₃

This is an ordinary weighted sum again — the exact same normal equation and gradient descent machinery from Lecture 2 apply unchanged. "Polynomial" describes the curved shape in x; "linear regression" describes how the weights get solved — two different questions, both true at once.

Proof, not just a claim — same data, two axes

A real relationship: y = x² + 1. Plot y against x, and it's a curve. Plot the exact same y against z = x², and it's a dead straight line.
