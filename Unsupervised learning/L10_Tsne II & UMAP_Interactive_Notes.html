Recap — everything up to P

Class 09 stopped the instant P was finished. Before building anything new, walk the whole chain once, properly, so nothing here is taken on faith.

the chain we built last class
$$\text{High-D data } X \;\to\; \text{distances} \;\to\; \text{Gaussian similarity} \;\to\; \text{normalize} \;\to\; p_{j|i} \;\to\; (\text{pick } \sigma_i \text{ via perplexity}) \;\to\; P$$
1

High-D data. Every point xᵢ lives in the original space — 100 dimensions, say, for 10,000 customers. We can't look at this directly.

2

Distances. For every pair (i, j), compute the ordinary Euclidean distance d(i,j) = ‖xᵢ − xⱼ‖. Same formula as always — just in n dimensions instead of 2 or 3.

3

Gaussian similarity. Distance points the wrong way (small = close), so we flip it with a Gaussian: $\text{similarity}(i,j) = e^{-\|x_i-x_j\|^2/2\sigma_i^2}$. Bounded at 1, decays smoothly to 0, and — crucially — has one adjustable width, σᵢ, that belongs to point i alone.

4

Normalize. Raw similarity scores don't sum to 1, so we divide each by the row total: $p_{j|i} = \dfrac{e^{-\|x_i-x_j\|^2/2\sigma_i^2}}{\sum_{k\neq i} e^{-\|x_i-x_k\|^2/2\sigma_i^2}}$ — now a genuine per-point probability distribution over "who is my neighbor."

5

Pick σᵢ via perplexity. Rather than guessing σᵢ, we fix one interpretable number — perplexity, "how many neighbors should matter" — compute entropy $H(P_i)=-\sum_j p_{j|i}\log_2 p_{j|i}$, convert it via $\text{Perplexity}(P_i)=2^{H(P_i)}$, and binary-search for whichever σᵢ makes that number match our chosen target. This runs once, independently, for every single point.

6

P. The collection of every point's own $p_{j|i}$ row. This is everything Class 09 built — a complete, high-fidelity map of neighborhood structure in the original space.

The running example we'll keep using all classSix points, two clusters, real 3D coordinates — identical to Class 09's worked example, so every number today builds on numbers you've already seen: A(0,0,0), B(0.5,0.3,0.2), C(0.3,0.6,0.1) form a tight "dense" cluster; D(8,8,8), E(9,7,8.5), F(7.5,8.5,7) form a "sparse" cluster roughly 13 units away. 01 · the dial, made completely visual

Perplexity, made completely visual

One more pass at perplexity — this time purely through pictures, so the idea is unmistakable before we move on.

Picture two neighborhoods sitting in the same high-dimensional space:

Same target perplexity for the gold point in both panels — but reaching it takes a small σ on the left (crowded) and a large σ on the right (empty). The dashed gold circle is literally "how far this point's Gaussian has to stretch."

Now the payoff, stated as plainly as possible: perplexity never asks "how far is far?" It only asks "how many points does your probability distribution treat as real neighbors?" — and it is σᵢ's entire job to make that true, however far or near those real neighbors physically sit.

p(·|A) — dense point, σ_A = 2.442. All the weight sits on its two true cluster-mates.
p(·|D) — sparse point, σ_D = 2.832 (a noticeably bigger σ). Nearly identical shape of distribution, on a completely different physical scale.
The one sentence to keep foreverPerplexity fixes the shape of the distribution (how concentrated vs. spread the probability mass is); σᵢ is whatever number makes that shape happen, and that number is free to be wildly different for two points that are both "correctly" reaching the same target. That's the entire reason σ is per-point and perplexity is global.

03 · the asymmetry nobody expects

One relationship, two numbers: p(B|A) vs. p(A|B)

Take two points, A and B, sitting some distance apart in high-D space.

We can compute two different conditional probabilities for this one pair:

p(B|A)

"Standing at A, and looking only at A's own neighborhood scale σ_A, how strongly is B a neighbor?"

p(A|B)

"Standing at B, and looking only at B's own neighborhood scale σ_B, how strongly is A a neighbor?"

These two numbers don't have to agree — and usually don'tBecause $\sigma_A \neq \sigma_B$ in general, A and B each judge the same physical distance through a different lens. A might sit in a dense crowd (small σ_A, so B — however close — competes with many other close points for probability mass), while B might sit in a sparser area (larger σ_B, so A stands out as relatively more important to B than B is to A).

Our real data shows this directly:

04 · optional, self-read — the full derivation

Why divide by 2n? optional

The natural first instinct is to just average the two directions. That's almost right — this section works out precisely why "almost" isn't good enough, and what the exact fix is.

Step 1 — the naive average

Suppose $p_{B|A}=0.8$ and $p_{A|B}=0.3$. The obvious combination:

$$p_{AB} \overset{?}{=} \dfrac{p_{B|A}+p_{A|B}}{2} = \dfrac{0.8+0.3}{2} = 0.55$$

This feels reasonable — "A's view + B's view → one symmetric relationship" — and it does fix the asymmetry: now $p_{AB}=p_{BA}$, there's a single number, done. So why does t-SNE not stop here?

Step 2 — check whether it's still a probability distribution

A valid joint probability distribution over all pairs must sum to exactly 1 — that was the entire reason we normalized back in Class 09's Section 12. Check it on our real 6-point data: if we only average (divide by 2, never by n), and sum every off-diagonal entry:

$$\sum_{i \neq j} \dfrac{p_{j|i}+p_{i|j}}{2} = 6.000 \qquad \text{— not 1. It's exactly } n.$$
Why it lands exactly on n, not by accidentEach of the n rows of P individually sums to 1 (that's what normalization in Class 09 guaranteed): $\sum_{j\neq i} p_{j|i} = 1$ for every row i. Averaging two matrices that are each "row-stochastic" and then summing every entry of the result just adds up n independent 1's — landing on exactly n, every time, for any dataset. Simple averaging can never produce a valid joint distribution over pairs; it's structurally guaranteed to overshoot by a factor of n.

Step 3 — the actual fix: divide by 2n, not 2

t-SNE's real symmetrization formula
$$p_{ij} = \dfrac{p_{j|i}+p_{i|j}}{2n}$$

Same numerator as the naive average — we still genuinely combine both directions — but now divided by 2n instead of just 2. Redo the sum with this correction, on the real data (n = 6):

QuantityValue
$\sum_{i\neq j}(p_{j|i}+p_{i|j})$12.000 (= 2n, always, by the row-stochastic argument above)
Divide by 2n = 1212.000 / 12 = 1.000 ✓
Verified on the real matrixSumming every one of the 30 off-diagonal entries of our real symmetrized $P$ (6 points, 2 per row × 6 rows counted... every ordered pair) gives exactly 1.0000000000000002 — a genuine probability distribution over the entire set of pairs at once, not 6 separate per-point distributions any more.

Step 4 — what "if not" actually looks like

If you skip the n and only divide by 2 (the naive average), here's the concrete failure: gradient descent later on will use these $p_{ij}$ values directly as targets. If they sum to n instead of 1, every single one of them is n-times too large — the KL divergence loss (Section 11) stops being a valid divergence between two probability distributions altogether, because one side (Q) sums to 1 and the other (the un-fixed P) sums to n. The comparison becomes meaningless the moment either side isn't a real distribution.

05 · switching gears

P is done — the new goal

Every number from here on is about a completely different object.

the question this section answers, precisely
$$X \in \mathbb{R}^{10{,}000 \times 100} \;\longrightarrow\; Y \in \mathbb{R}^{10{,}000 \times 2}$$

We started with 10,000 points in 100 dimensions. $P$ — everything built so far — is a $10{,}000 \times 10{,}000$ table of relationships computed from that original data. But $P$ is not itself a picture. We still need an actual 2D point for every single one of those 10,000 original points.

ObjectShapeWhat it holds
X10,000 × 100the original data — untouched, still sitting in 100D
P10,000 × 10,000relationships computed from X — "who should be near whom"
Y10,000 × 2the thing we still have to create — one new 2D coordinate per original point
A distinction worth being precise aboutPCA computes $Z = XW$ — a fixed, one-shot mathematical projection of the original coordinates onto new axes. t-SNE does something structurally different: it never projects X anywhere. It only ever used X to compute distances, which fed into $P$. From here on, $X$ is done being useful — t-SNE manufactures a brand new set of 2D coordinates $Y=\{y_1,\dots,y_n\}$ from scratch and then moves them, repeatedly, until they satisfy $P$. Nothing about $Y$'s starting position needs to bear any resemblance to $X$'s geometry.
what a single row looks like
$$x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{100} \quad\longrightarrow\quad y_i \in \mathbb{R}^{2} \qquad \text{e.g. } x_1 \to y_1=(2.1,\ 0.7)$$

If there are 10,000 original points, t-SNE will create 10,000 new 2D points — one per row. The only question left before any optimization can start: where do these 2D points initially come from?

06 · the starting position

Initializing Y — random vs. PCA

t-SNE does not know in advance where the 2D points should go. It has to start somewhere and then let gradient descent do the work. There are two standard ways to pick that starting point.

1 · Random initialization

Every $y_i$ is drawn from a small random distribution — typically $y_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 10^{-4})$ — placing all 10,000 points in a tiny, essentially structureless cloud near the origin. No information from $X$ is used at all; it is a pure guess.

2 · PCA initialization t-SNE's default

Run ordinary PCA on $X$, keep only the top 2 principal components, and use that 2D projection as the starting $Y$. This injects real, if imperfect, global structure from the very first step — same PCA machinery from Class 08–09, just now used as a warm-start rather than a final answer.

Say it plainlyPCA initialization is the default in modern t-SNE implementations (including scikit-learn since recent versions). Random initialization still exists and is occasionally used deliberately (e.g. to check that a result isn't an artifact of one particular starting layout), but if you don't specify otherwise, you are getting the PCA-based start.

07 · P's mirror image

From Y to Q — similarity in low dimensions

Once $Y$ exists (random or PCA-initialized), we can ask the exact same question we asked in high-D: "given where these 2D points currently sit, how similar are they to each other?" That answer is called $Q$.

CoordinatesX (10,000 × 100)Y (10,000 × 2)

The picture to hold in your head$P$ is fixed — it was computed once from the original data and never changes again. $Q$ is alive — every time gradient descent moves a point in $Y$, every entry of $Q$ can change. The entire rest of t-SNE is: compare $P$ (fixed target) against $Q$ (moving current state), and nudge $Y$ so $Q$ drifts toward $P$.

The obvious first guess: reuse the exact same Gaussian formula that built $P$.

the tempting (but ultimately wrong) reuse
$$\text{similarity}(i,j) \overset{?}{=} e^{-\|y_i-y_j\|^2/2\sigma^2}$$

We could do this mathematically — nothing stops us. But t-SNE deliberately does not use a Gaussian on the low-D side. It switches to a completely different distribution, and that switch is not a stylistic choice — it exists to fix a specific, geometric problem that shows up only once you actually try to squeeze high-D relationships into 2D. That problem is the crowding problem.

08 · the problem that forces a new distribution

The crowding problem

Before any formula: a simple geometric fact that has nothing to do with t-SNE specifically. It's just what happens when you compress space.

Take four points arranged as a perfect square in 2D, side length 2:

A perfect 2D square: four equal sides of length 2, and two diagonals of length 2.83 — six pairwise distances, only two distinct values.

Now try to lay these same four points on a single 1D line — squeezing one dimension away, exactly what t-SNE does going from 100D (or even just 3D) down to 2D:

Place A, B, C correctly spaced (2 apart each). D needs to be simultaneously 2 away from A, 2.83 away from B, and 2 away from C — impossible on a single line. Something has to give.
This is the crowding problem, stated plainlyA 1D line simply does not have enough "room" to honor all six of the square's pairwise distances at once. Wherever you place D, at least one of its distances gets distorted — usually by getting squashed too close to its neighbors, because a lower-dimensional space runs out of directions to spread points apart in. You cannot faithfully locate all the data points from a higher dimension inside a lower one — some relationships are forced to crowd together that weren't crowded before.
Why this gets dramatically worse from 100D → 2D, not just 2D → 1DThe kissing number — the maximum number of equidistant neighbors a single point can have — grows explosively with dimension: 2 in 2D, 12 in 3D, 24 in 4D, and 240 in 8D. In a 100-dimensional space, a point can have an enormous number of genuinely equidistant "close" neighbors at once. Force that same point down into 2D, where only 6 points can ever be mutually equidistant from it, and most of those neighbors are mathematically forced to crowd into the same small region — there simply isn't enough room in 2D to keep them all appropriately spread out and distinct.

Why a Gaussian on the low-D side makes crowding worse, not better

A Gaussian similarity decays very fast — it treats "moderately far" almost the same as "extremely far" (both ≈ 0). Watch what that means once points are forced into crowded 2D space:

We need the low-D similarity function to stay meaningfully non-zero for longer, so points that end up moderately far apart (forced there by crowding) still get a usable, distinguishable similarity value. That is precisely what the Student-t distribution provides.
09 · optional, self-read — end to end

The Student-t distribution optional

Everything about why t-SNE uses this specific curve on the low-D side, from its formula to its name to its shape — none of this is required to use or explain t-SNE at a working level, but it removes any remaining mystery around the "t" in t-SNE.

t-SNE's low-D similarity function
$$\text{similarity}(i,j) = \dfrac{1}{1+\|y_i-y_j\|^2}$$

This is the Student-t distribution's density shape with exactly one degree of freedom ($\nu=1$) — the specific special case of the Student-t family that is simple enough to have this clean closed form, with no exponential anywhere in it.

QuestionAnswer
What is the Student-t distribution, generally?A family of bell-shaped probability distributions, controlled by a "degrees of freedom" parameter $\nu$. Originally derived to describe the sampling distribution of a mean estimated from a small sample, where the true variance is unknown and must itself be estimated.
Where does the name come from?Statistician William Sealy Gosset developed it while working at the Guinness brewery, and published it in 1908 under the pen name "Student" (Guinness restricted employees from publishing under their own names) — hence "Student's t-distribution."
How does it differ from a Gaussian, shape-wise?Both are symmetric and bell-shaped, but the Student-t has heavier tails — it assigns meaningfully more probability to values far from the center. As $\nu \to \infty$, the Student-t converges exactly to the standard Gaussian; smaller $\nu$ means heavier tails.
Why df = 1, specifically?$\nu=1$ is the heaviest-tailed member of the whole Student-t family (this special case is also called the Cauchy distribution). t-SNE wants the heaviest reasonable tails available, to solve crowding as aggressively as possible — so it goes straight to the extreme end of the family, and gets a bonus: at $\nu=1$ the formula collapses to the very simple $\frac{1}{1+d^2}$, with no exponential to evaluate, which is also computationally cheaper at scale.

Why doesn't Q need a symmetrization step like P did?

Recall exactly why $P$ needed fixing in Sections 03–04: $p_{j|i}$ and $p_{i|j}$ used two different denominators, because they were each normalized using a different point's own $\sigma$ ($\sigma_i$ vs $\sigma_j$) — that mismatch is what created two disagreeing numbers for one relationship in the first place.

11 · optional, self-read — from first principles

KL divergence optional

We now have two full probability distributions over the exact same set of pairs — $P$ (what we want) and $Q$ (what we currently have). We need one single number that says how different they are. That number is the Kullback–Leibler divergence.

Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
$$KL(P\|Q) = \sum_{i\neq j} p_{ij}\,\log\dfrac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}$$

Can KL divergence ever be negative?

Individual terms in the sum absolutely can be negative (see the table above, row 3). The question is whether the total, summed over every pair, can ever go negative. It cannot — and this is provable, not just observed.

Why the total is always ≥ 0 (Gibbs' inequality)This is a general mathematical fact about any two valid probability distributions, not specific to t-SNE. Intuition: $-\log(x)$ is a convex function, and Jensen's inequality for a convex function applied to the expectation $\mathbb{E}_p\!\left[-\log\frac{q}{p}\right]$ gives $KL(P\|Q) = \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\log\frac{p}{q}\right] \geq -\log\left(\mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\frac{q}{p}\right]\right) = -\log\left(\sum_i q_i\right) = -\log(1) = 0$, using the fact that $Q$ also sums to exactly 1. Equality holds only when $P$ and $Q$ are identical everywhere.
the bound, stated simply
$$KL(P\|Q) \geq 0, \qquad \text{with equality if and only if } P = Q \text{ everywhere}$$
So — negative or positive, overall?Individual pair-wise terms can go either way (positive when Q underestimates, negative when Q overestimates a low-weight pair) — but they can never combine into a negative total. $KL(P\|Q)$ behaves exactly like a "distance": always zero or positive, zero only for a perfect match, and it grows the more $Q$ departs from $P$ on the pairs $P$ considers important. (Technically it is not a true mathematical distance — it isn't symmetric, $KL(P\|Q)\neq KL(Q\|P)$ in general — but it behaves like one for our purposes: something to drive toward zero.)
12 · the choice that shapes everything

Why minimize KL(P‖Q), and not the reverse?

t-SNE's loss function is $KL(P\|Q)$ — P first, Q second. This ordering is not arbitrary, and swapping it produces a genuinely different (worse) algorithm.

t-SNE's actual objective
$$\min_{Y} KL(P\|Q) = \min_Y \sum_{i\neq j} p_{ij}\log\dfrac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}$$

Because each term is weighted by $p_{ij}$ (not $q_{ij}$), the loss only really "cares" about pairs that $P$ says are important — i.e., pairs that were genuinely close in the original high-D space.

If P says "close" (large p_ij) but Q says "far" (small q_ij)

$p_{ij}/q_{ij}$ is large, $\log(\cdot)$ is large and positive, and it's multiplied by a large weight $p_{ij}$ — a big penalty. This is exactly the mistake we most want to punish: losing a real neighbor.

If P says "far" (tiny p_ij) but Q says "close" (larger q_ij)

$p_{ij}/q_{ij}$ is small, $\log(\cdot)$ is negative — but multiplied by a tiny weight $p_{ij}\approx 0$, so the contribution to the total is negligible regardless of how wrong Q is.

In one sentence$KL(P\|Q)$ is heavily penalized for failing to preserve true neighbors (false negatives), but almost indifferent to accidentally placing two unrelated points near each other in 2D (false positives). t-SNE, by design, cares enormously more about the first kind of mistake than the second.

What the reverse, KL(Q‖P), would optimize for instead

$$KL(Q\|P) = \sum_{i\neq j} q_{ij}\log\dfrac{q_{ij}}{p_{ij}}$$

Now every term is weighted by $q_{ij}$ — the current, 2D-based similarity — instead of the target $p_{ij}$.

That's precisely the trade-off a good cluster-visualization tool should make.
13 · from loss to motion

Gradient descent — the update rule

We now have a number, $KL(P\|Q)$, that tells us how wrong the current 2D layout is. The only question left: how do we actually change the 2D coordinates to make that number smaller?

the question, and its one-word answer
$$\text{"How do we reduce } KL(P\|Q) \text{?"} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \textbf{Gradient Descent}$$

The idea, in the simplest possible words: the gradient of a loss function points in the direction that makes the loss increase fastest. So to decrease the loss, move every coordinate a small step in exactly the opposite direction.

the update rule
$$Y_{\text{new}} = Y_{\text{old}} - \eta \, \nabla_Y KL(P\|Q)$$
SymbolMeaning
Yall the 2D coordinates, all n points at once
∇_Y KLthe gradient of the KL loss with respect to those coordinates — one 2D vector per point, telling that point which way to move
ηthe learning rate — how big a step to take each iteration

Working this out (calculus, not shown — the derivation isn't required, only the result and what it means) gives a specific, computable formula for the gradient at a single point $i$:

the t-SNE gradient
$$\dfrac{\partial\, KL(P\|Q)}{\partial y_i} = 4\sum_{j} (p_{ij}-q_{ij})(y_i-y_j)\big(1+\|y_i-y_j\|^2\big)^{-1}$$
The one thing worth extracting from this formula right nowDon't memorize it — the important message is structural: the gradient at point $i$ is a sum, over every other point $j$, of a term that depends on (p_ij − q_ij) — exactly how wrong the current relationship between $i$ and $j$ is — times the direction $(y_i - y_j)$ between them. Section 14 unpacks exactly what that sign does.

the five things to remember
1. Distance in both spaces is (usually) ordinary Euclidean distance.
2. High-D similarity uses a Gaussian — bounded, smooth, one σ per point.
3. Low-D similarity uses a Student-t (df=1) — heavier tails, fixes crowding.
4. P = original relationships (fixed target). Q = current 2D relationships (moves every step).
5. Minimize KL(P‖Q) by moving the 2D coordinates via gradient descent.
The complete t-SNE computation graph, top to bottom, with the loop at the very end feeding back into itself until convergence.
The one-paragraph version, worth memorizing in your own words

t-SNE first describes the original high-dimensional data purely in terms of neighbor probabilities — Gaussian similarity, tuned per point via a single global perplexity setting, then symmetrized into one probability distribution $P$ over pairs. It then manufactures a fresh set of 2D points, starting either from noise or a PCA projection, and describes their relationships the same way — but with a heavier-tailed Student-t distribution instead of a Gaussian, specifically to leave room for the crowding that's unavoidable when compressing very high-dimensional structure into just two dimensions. It measures the gap between the two descriptions with KL divergence — a loss that punishes losing true neighbors far more than it punishes accidental 2D closeness — and repeatedly nudges the 2D points with gradient descent, attracting true neighbors together and repelling unrelated points apart, until the two descriptions agree as closely as they're going to.

The promise from Class 09, delivered — why "t-SNE"?Class 09 unpacked three of the four words in "t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding" and asked you to hold onto the fourth until this class. Now that the whole picture is built: Neighbor — every part of this, from $P$ to $Q$ to the gradient, is about who's close to whom, never about raw coordinates. Stochastic — $P$ and $Q$ are literally probability distributions, and different random initializations (Section 06) can lead to different, if locally-similar, final layouts. Embedding — the same data, represented fresh in a new, lower-dimensional space. And now, finally: t-distributed — the low-dimensional side, $Q$, is built with a Student-t distribution (df=1) instead of a Gaussian (Sections 07–09), specifically to solve the crowding problem (Section 08). Put the four words together and the name stops being a label to memorize: t-SNE embeds neighbor relationships stochastically, using a t-distribution to describe the low-D side.
17 · the bridge to something newer

Limitations of t-SNE

t-SNE is powerful, but everything about how it works (Sections 00–16) has direct practical costs. These are exactly what motivated UMAP.

Slow at scale

Computing full pairwise distances, then a binary search per point, then a gradient over every pair, every iteration — this is roughly $O(n^2)$ per iteration in its plain form. On tens or hundreds of thousands of points, this gets genuinely slow.

No natural way to embed new points

t-SNE optimizes the 2D coordinates $Y$ directly, for the specific dataset it was run on. There is no built-in $X\to Y$ function to reuse on a brand-new point later — a fresh point normally means rerunning the whole algorithm.

Global distances aren't meaningful

Recall from Class 09 (Section 05): two different t-SNE runs preserve local neighbors well but land in different rotations. Relatedly, the size of the gap between two distant clusters, or a cluster's absolute size on the page, isn't reliably meaningful — only relative local closeness is.

Sensitive to perplexity, and to initialization

Class 09, Section 15 showed real pictures where too-low or too-high perplexity visibly fractures or blurs true structure. Different random initializations can also converge to meaningfully different-looking (if locally-similar) layouts.

A practical-vs-guaranteed distinction worth holding ontoSome of these are usually true in practice (t-SNE runs slower than UMAP on most real datasets) without being mathematically guaranteed in every case; others (no natural out-of-sample extension in the plain formulation) are structural. UMAP's design directly targets several of these — but, as Section 18 will show, it doesn't erase every trade-off, it makes different ones.

Meet UMAP — the high-level flow

UMAP = Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection. Same job as t-SNE — dimensionality reduction, primarily for visualization — built on a different mathematical foundation (topology and graph theory rather than probability-distribution matching), aimed squarely at t-SNE's weak points. (What those four words actually mean will make far more sense once you've seen the whole flow — that reveal is waiting at the end of Section 20.)

the shape of the problem UMAP is still solving
$$\text{High-D data} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{"who is a neighbor of whom?"} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{a 2D/3D layout that keeps neighbors close}$$

That's identical to t-SNE. What changes is how "who is a neighbor" gets represented, and how the two spaces get compared.

The two knobs you actually turn in practiceEverything in UMAP is controlled through two hyperparameters — introduced here at a high level, fully unpacked with real numbers in Section 19: n_neighbors (how many neighbors each point connects to when building $G_{high}$ — small values chase tight local detail, large values pull in more global structure — t-SNE's perplexity plays almost exactly this role) and min_dist (how tightly points are allowed to pack together in the final low-D plot — small values produce tight, dense clusters, large values spread everything out more evenly).
The one-paragraph version to actually rememberUMAP asks the same question as t-SNE — preserve neighbors, not variance — but answers it by building an explicit graph of nearest-neighbor connections in high-D, building the same kind of graph in low-D, and pushing the low-D graph to match the high-D one using cross-entropy instead of KL divergence. The k-nearest-neighbor and sparse-graph structure, plus negative sampling during optimization, are what make UMAP noticeably faster than t-SNE on large datasets, and give it a natural (if still imperfect) way to place a new point without rerunning everything.

Step 1 — find each point's neighbors (hyperparameter: n_neighbors)

For every point, look at the original high-D data and find its n_neighbors closest points (commonly 5–50 in real use; we use $k=3$ here, matching t-SNE's target perplexity = 2 on this same tiny dataset). Think of it like deciding, at a party, how many people count as "close enough to matter": say 3, and you get small, sharply-defined friend groups; say 50, and you get a blurrier, more "everyone's sort of connected" sense of the whole room. Small n_neighbors → sharp local detail (but can fragment bigger structures); large n_neighbors → a smoother global shape (but can blur fine detail). Same underlying trade-off as t-SNE's perplexity, different mechanism underneath.

Step 2 — decide how far "close" is, per point

Not every point's neighborhood looks the same size — a point buried in a dense cluster has neighbors right on top of it; a point out in a sparse patch has to reach much further to find any. UMAP handles this with two per-point numbers, $\rho_i$ and $\sigma_i$:

two per-point numbers, both purely local
$$\rho_i = \min_j\, d(x_i, x_j) \qquad \text{(distance to i's single nearest neighbor)}$$

$\rho_i$ is a guarantee, not something to compute by hand: whoever a point's single closest neighbor is, that neighbor always counts as a full-strength (weight = 1) connection — no point is ever left disconnected just because its neighborhood happens to be sparse. $\sigma_i$ is then found the same way t-SNE finds its per-point $\sigma$ (Class 09) — a quick per-point search — and controls how fast connection strength fades out for the 2nd, 3rd, ... neighbor. That's the real substance behind "graph" vs. "dense probability matrix" from Section 18's table — and it's exactly why UMAP's per-step cost stays close to $O(n)$ instead of t-SNE's $O(n^2)$.

Step 4 — combine both directions into one graph (symmetrize)

Just like t-SNE, $w_{i|j}$ and $w_{j|i}$ can disagree — each was built using its own point's ρ and σ. UMAP's fix is structurally different from t-SNE's $\div 2n$ average:

the probabilistic OR
$$w_{ij} = w_{i|j} + w_{j|i} - w_{i|j}\cdot w_{j|i}$$
This is the formula for the union of two probabilities (treating each weight as "the probability this edge exists"): "connected from either direction" rather than "the average strength of both directions." It guarantees the combined weight is at least as large as either individual weight — an edge survives if either point considers the other a neighbor, rather than being diluted by averaging against a possibly-low reverse weight.

Step 5 — give the 2D layout a smart starting position

UMAP doesn't start from random noise, and it doesn't start from PCA either (that's t-SNE's move, Section 06) — it starts from a layout built directly out of the graph from Step 4. The method is called spectral embedding, and the intuition is simpler than the name sounds: imagine every edge in the graph is a rubber band, pulled tight in proportion to its weight — near-1.0 edges (true neighbors) pull hard, the two razor-thin 0.0025/0.0098 cross-cluster threads barely pull at all. Let the six points settle into whatever low-energy resting position that tug-of-war naturally produces. That settled position — computed once, directly, with no trial and error — is where gradient descent gets to start from, instead of starting blind.

The real, computed spectral-init layout: the two clusters have already separated to opposite sides before any gradient descent runs at all — the same "free head start" story as PCA init for t-SNE (Section 06), just built from the graph instead of from raw variance.
How this compares to t-SNEThis plays exactly the role PCA-init played for t-SNE (Section 06) — a free, structure-aware head start before real optimization begins — just built from the connection graph instead of from raw variance. Both exist for the same reason: a good starting point converges faster and more reliably than starting blind, and both are the library default for exactly that reason.

Step 6 — build G_low, and control spacing with min_dist

With a low-D layout in hand, UMAP needs a similarity curve for the 2D side too — its analogue of t-SNE's Student-t. But it adds a control t-SNE simply doesn't have: min_dist, literally "how close am I allowed to let two points sit, at minimum, in the final picture." Think of it as a personal-space rule at a party: a small min_dist says "stand shoulder-to-shoulder if you want" — dense, tightly-packed clusters; a large min_dist says "keep some visible space, even from your best friend" — a looser, more evenly-spread-out picture. Nothing about which points are neighbors changes — only how tightly the final picture is allowed to pack them together.

All three curves start at weight 1 and decay to 0 — but a larger min_dist makes the curve drop off sooner, at shorter distances, forcing points apart more aggressively in the final embedding. A smaller min_dist lets the curve stay near 1 for longer, permitting much tighter, denser-looking clusters.

Practically: a smaller min_dist (the library default is 0.1) tends to produce the tight, "blobby" clusters UMAP is often recognized by; pushing it up trades that tightness for a more evenly-spread layout — useful when you also care about structure within a cluster, not just which points belong to it.

The exact recipe, for anyone curious — again, optional and not interview material:

the target shape, before any fitting
$$f(d) = \begin{cases} 1 & d \le \text{min\_dist} \\ \exp\big(-(d-\text{min\_dist})\big) & d > \text{min\_dist} \end{cases}$$

Points closer than min_dist apart are treated as maximally similar (weight exactly 1, no further reward for squeezing them even closer); beyond that, similarity decays smoothly. UMAP then fits a smooth two-parameter curve to approximate this piecewise shape (smooth curves are much easier to differentiate for gradient descent than a curve with a sharp corner in it):

the fitted low-D similarity curve
$$w'_{ij} = \dfrac{1}{1+a\,\|y_i-y_j\|^{2b}}$$

$a$ and $b$ are found by least-squares curve-fitting against the piecewise target above — real numbers, computed here with scipy.optimize.curve_fit, for three different min_dist settings:

Step 7 — optimize with negative sampling

Computing the full cross-entropy sum over every possible pair, every iteration, would be just as expensive as t-SNE. Instead, at each step UMAP updates real edges plus a small random sample of "negative" (non-edge) pairs — borrowed directly from the same trick that makes word2vec fast — turning an $O(n^2)$-per-step problem into something closer to $O(n)$.

Now that the whole flow is built: what does "UMAP" actually mean?

Section 18 gave you the expansion up front — Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection — as a label to hang the algorithm on. Now that you've walked through every step, here's what each word is actually pointing at:

WordWhat it's actually referring to
ManifoldThe working assumption that your high-D data isn't scattered randomly through every dimension — it actually lies on (or near) some lower-dimensional curved surface tucked inside that high-D space. The graph-building steps (1–4) are an attempt to trace out that hidden surface using only the neighbor connections that can actually be observed.
UniformThe specific assumption that data is spread evenly across that manifold, if you measure distance the right way — exactly why $\rho_i$ (Step 2) rescales every point's neighborhood using its own local distances rather than one global scale. Locally, around any single point, the neighborhood is treated as if it were uniform.
ApproximationThe true manifold is never directly visible — only finitely many sampled points from it are. The fuzzy graph built in Steps 1–4 ($G_{high}$) is UMAP's best approximation of that unseen manifold's structure, built entirely from those samples.
ProjectionThe final stretch (Steps 5–7): taking that approximated high-D structure and finding a low-dimensional layout — starting from spectral initialization, refined by gradient descent — that projects it down into a 2D or 3D picture you can actually look at.
One sentence, now that it's earnedUMAP approximates the shape of the (assumed locally-uniform) manifold your data lives on, using a neighbor graph, then projects that approximated shape down into a low-dimensional picture — which is exactly the seven steps you just walked through, given its full name.
21 · zooming all the way out

When do you even need dimensionality reduction?

Before choosing PCA, t-SNE, or UMAP — the more basic question: what problem is dimensionality reduction actually solving?

You need to see the data

Nobody can look at a 100-dimensional scatter plot. Any time the goal is a human looking at a picture to sanity-check structure, spot outliers, or explain results to stakeholders, you need to get down to 2D or 3D.

You want to fight the curse of dimensionality

In very high dimensions, distances between points become less meaningful, models overfit more easily, and training gets slower. Reducing dimensions first can make downstream models more stable and faster.

Your features are redundant or correlated

Many real feature sets have heavy redundancy (multiple sensors measuring related quantities, many one-hot columns, etc.). A compact lower-dimensional representation can carry almost all the real signal.

You want to denoise before clustering or classifying

Keeping only the dimensions that carry real structure (PCA's top components, or a well-tuned manifold embedding) can strip out noise dimensions that would otherwise confuse a downstream algorithm.

The one thing to never forget from Classes 08–10All three algorithms in this module answer the question "fewer dimensions, but keep what?" differently: PCA keeps global variance. t-SNE and UMAP keep local neighbor relationships. That single distinction (first raised all the way back in Class 09, Section 03) is what should drive every choice from here on.
22 · inside a real ML pipeline, part 1

Using it inside supervised learning

Supervised learning means you eventually need to predict — on brand-new data the model has never seen. That single requirement rules some of today's tools in, and some out.

the scenario
$$X \;(\text{500 features}) \;\longrightarrow\; \text{predict } y$$

500 raw features might be slow to train on, mostly redundant, or simply too many relative to your row count. The fix: reduce to a smaller set of dimensions before handing features to the model — but this has to be done correctly with respect to your train/validation/test split.

The one rule that matters: fit (learn the projection / manifold) only on training data; every other split only ever calls transform with that already-fitted object.
Why fitting on val/test is a real bug, not a style preferenceIf you re-fit PCA (or anything else) on your validation or test set, the reduced features for those rows now depend on information (their own variance, their own axes) that a real, deployed model would never have access to at prediction time. This is data leakage — it can make validation/test metrics look better than the model will actually perform in production, because the transformation itself "peeked" at those rows.

23 · inside a real ML pipeline, part 2

Using it inside unsupervised learning

Unsupervised learning has no labels to protect from leakage, so the trap here is different: confusing "a nice-looking picture" with "the actual clustering."

Scenario 1 — cluster first, visualize second recommended
Run clustering (K-Means, DBSCAN, GMM, …) on the original data or a PCA-reduced version of it. Evaluate the clustering properly (silhouette score, WCSS/elbow, domain judgment). Then use t-SNE or UMAP purely to draw a 2D picture of those already-decided cluster labels, to show someone.
vs
Scenario 2 — cluster directly on a t-SNE/UMAP embedding use with caution
Run t-SNE or UMAP down to 2D first, then run K-Means (or eyeball it) directly on those 2 coordinates. Visually persuasive, but risky: neither method reliably preserves relative cluster density or size, and both can visually manufacture apparent separation that isn't really there, or merge genuinely distinct groups.
Why Scenario 2 is risky, concretelyRecall Class 09, Section 05: two different t-SNE runs, same data, preserved local neighbor structure well (~74% fidelity) but landed in visually different global arrangements — and neither run's inter-cluster gaps or cluster sizes are meant to be read literally. A K-Means run directly on 2D t-SNE/UMAP coordinates is clustering on a representation that was never optimized to preserve the specific things K-Means depends on (Euclidean density and relative distances, globally) — it was only optimized to preserve local neighbor identity.
If asked directly: "if two points land close together in the 2D plot, does that mean they're in the same cluster?"Locally: usually yes — that's the one thing both algorithms are explicitly built to preserve. But the reverse inference is unsafe: two points landing far apart doesn't necessarily mean they were far apart originally (crowding, Section 08, can force this), and the visual size of the gap between two clusters carries little to no reliable meaning in either method. Treat the picture as strong evidence for "these two specific points are related," and weak evidence for anything about absolute distances or gaps.

Everything, one last time

Sections 00–02
Revised the full high-D pipeline (distance → Gaussian → normalize → σᵢ via perplexity → P), then made perplexity completely visual: same target perplexity, wildly different σ for a dense point (2.442) vs. a sparse one (2.832), same resulting distribution shape.
Sections 03–04
p(j|i) ≠ p(i|j) in general, because each direction is normalized with a different point's own σ. The fix isn't a simple average — dividing by 2n (not just 2) is what's required to make the symmetrized P a genuine probability distribution over pairs, summing to exactly 1.
Sections 05–06
With P finished, the real remaining job is manufacturing a brand-new 2D coordinate Y for every point — never a projection of X. Random initialization is a pure guess; PCA initialization (the default) starts from real global structure and, verified on real numbers, converges dramatically faster.
Sections 07–10
A Gaussian on the low-D side would make the unavoidable "crowding problem" worse (illustrated with a 2D square that can't be faithfully placed on a 1D line). Student-t with 1 degree of freedom — the heaviest-tailed member of its family — keeps moderately-far points meaningfully distinguishable instead of flattening them all to zero. Q needs no symmetrization step: one shared denominator, already-symmetric distances.
Sections 11–12
KL(P‖Q) measures the mismatch between the two distributions, is always ≥ 0 (Gibbs' inequality), and — because every term is weighted by the target p_ij — punishes losing real neighbors far more than it punishes accidental low-D closeness. That asymmetry is a deliberate design choice, not an accident.
Sections 13–16
Gradient descent moves each point according to sign(p_ij − q_ij): attraction when P wants more closeness than Q currently has, repulsion otherwise — verified directly with real attract/repel numbers. Looped to convergence, comparing real KL trajectories: PCA init reached KL < 0.05 in 2 steps vs. 34 for random init.
Sections 17–20
t-SNE's real costs (speed, no out-of-sample transform, sensitivity to perplexity/init) motivate UMAP: a k-NN graph instead of a dense probability matrix, fuzzy-set union instead of ÷2n averaging, and a two-term cross-entropy loss instead of KL divergence — verified with a real k=3 toy example on the same 6-point dataset.
Sections 21–26
Dimensionality reduction exists to let a human see the data, fight the curse of dimensionality, or denoise/compress. PCA is the safe default for supervised-learning preprocessing (it has a real, leakage-safe transform); t-SNE and UMAP are for visualization and EDA, with clustering ideally done in the original or PCA space first and t-SNE/UMAP used only to draw the picture afterward.
The one-paragraph version of the entire module

PCA finds the directions of maximum variance and gives you an exact, reusable, invertible projection — the right default whenever a downstream model needs stable numeric features. t-SNE throws variance away entirely and instead asks, point by point, "who are my real neighbors?" — turning that question into matching probability distributions (Gaussian in high-D, heavy-tailed Student-t in low-D) via gradient descent on KL divergence, at the cost of speed and any way to place new points afterward. UMAP asks the same neighbor question but represents it as a weighted graph instead of a probability distribution, symmetrizes it with a fuzzy-set union instead of an average, and optimizes a two-term cross-entropy with negative sampling — landing on something faster, more scalable, and with a genuine (if approximate) way to embed new points, while still keeping most of t-SNE's visual cluster quality. None of the three is strictly "better" — each one is the right tool once you know exactly which of "compress," "preserve local neighbors," or "preserve local neighbors at scale" is actually the job.
