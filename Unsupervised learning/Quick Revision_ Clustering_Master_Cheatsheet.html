algorithm 01 · hard · centroid-based

K-Means

Alternates between guessing where the centres are and re-averaging them toward whoever's actually nearby — repeating until nobody wants to switch sides. Guaranteed to converge because every step can only tighten clusters (lower WCSS), never loosen them.

  1. Choose K upfront — K-Means cannot spawn or delete clusters mid-run.
  2. Initialise K centroids (random, or smarter — see K-Means++ next).
  3. Assign: every point → nearest centroid (Euclidean distance).
  4. Update: each centroid → mean of its assigned points.
  5. Repeat assign/update until assignments stop changing.
core formulas $$d(p,q)=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^d (p_k-q_k)^2} \qquad WCSS=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{p\in k}\lVert p-C_k\rVert^2$$

WCSS = Inertia (sklearn's model.inertia_) = SSE. The mean provably minimises WCSS for a fixed membership — that's why K-Means always converges.

Choosing K

Order matters: Elbow (plot WCSS vs K, shortlist the bend) → Silhouette Score (confirm on that shortlist — see the Silhouette section) → business constraints last, as a deliberate override, never the starting point.

The trap: never pick K by minimising WCSS directly — it falls monotonically to exactly 0 at K=n (one cluster per point). The elbow, not the minimum, is the signal.

The three assumptions that quietly break K-Means

AssumptionBreaks onTry instead
Shape — convex/round clustersRings, crescents/moons, any non-globular structureDBSCAN
Size — similar territory per clusterTrue clusters of very different sizesHierarchical, GMM
Density — similar spread/varianceOne tight+dense cluster next to one loose+sparse oneDBSCAN, GMM
Why shape is geometry, not a design choice: the perpendicular bisector between two centroids is a straight line ⇒ the region closer to one centroid is a convex polygon. Swap in Manhattan distance and the boundary becomes a diamond — shape comes from the distance metric, not the data.

algorithm 02 · hard · smarter init + feature engineering

K-Means++ & RFM Segmentation

K-Means++ changes exactly one thing: how starting centroids are picked. Assignment, update, and convergence are unmodified, identical K-Means underneath.

  1. Pick the first centroid uniformly at random.
  2. For every remaining point, find its distance to the nearest already-chosen centroid — D(x).
  3. Square D(x), turn into probabilities, draw the next centroid by weighted random draw (roulette-wheel).
  4. Repeat 2–3 until K centroids are chosen.
selection probability $$P(x\text{ chosen}) = \dfrac{D(x)^2}{\sum_{x'} D(x')^2}$$
  • Why square D(x)? Widens the gap between near and far points — same reason WCSS itself squares distance.
  • Why MIN, once 2+ centroids exist? Targets genuinely underserved regions, not points merely far from one unrelated centroid.
Does K-Means++ guarantee the global optimum? No. The first pick is still random chance, and later picks are weighted, not forced — reliably better on average, not a guarantee. Production pairs it with multiple restarts (n_init).
Important: K-Means++ fixes where you start, not what a centroid can represent — the shape/size/density limits above still apply, even with restarts.

RFM — feature engineering before any clustering runs

Raw data is usually a transaction log (one row per line-item), not one row per customer. Building that one-row-per-entity table is RFM — most of the real thinking happens here, not in the K-Means call itself.

FeatureFormulaWhy this aggregation
Recencydays since customer's most recent purchaseReference date = max(date)+1, derived from data
Frequencycount of invoices per customerCaptures engagement independent of spend
Monetarysum (not average) of Quantity×UnitPriceSum reflects total value; average conflates "1 huge order" with "50 small ones"
  • IQR-clip outliers on all 3 columns before scaling (K-Means' mean-based centroid is outlier-sensitive), then StandardScaler — R/F/M have very different native ranges; unscaled, Monetary alone would dominate Euclidean distance.
  • Generalises beyond retail: aggregate raw events into one row per entity using business-relevant signals — telecom: usage/tenure/complaints · streaming: watch-time/completion-rate · banking: balance/txn-count.

Evaluating a cluster once formed — the priority order

  1. Geometric check — WCSS + Silhouette (cheap, catches broken clusterings).
  2. Profile the clusters — do mean R/F/M per group tell a coherent, human-nameable story?
  3. Stability check — rerun with a different seed / 80% subsample; do the groups persist?
  4. Business validation — does treating groups differently move a real metric? The only step with a real answer to "good enough for what?"

algorithm 03 · hard · no centroids, no upfront K

Hierarchical Clustering

Builds a complete tree of nested clusters instead of one flat partition — no centroids, no K chosen upfront. Agglomerative (bottom-up, used in practice): start with n singletons, merge the 2 closest, repeat for n−1 merges. Divisive (top-down, rare): start with 1 cluster, repeatedly split the highest-SSE cluster — usually via bisecting K-Means.

The one rule that never changes: whatever linkage you use, always merge the pair of clusters with the smallest computed distance.

Linkage methods

the four linkage formulas $$D_{\text{single}}(A,B)=\min_{a\in A,b\in B} d(a,b) \qquad D_{\text{complete}}(A,B)=\max_{a\in A,b\in B} d(a,b)$$ $$D_{\text{avg}}(A,B)=\frac{1}{|A||B|}\sum_{a\in A}\sum_{b\in B} d(a,b) \qquad \Delta_{\text{ward}}(A,B)=\frac{|A||B|}{|A|+|B|}\lVert\mu_A-\mu_B\rVert^2$$
LinkageBest forWatch out for
Single — nearest neighboursNon-convex, elongated, ring/crescent shapes"Chaining" through noise — welds separate blobs into one
Complete — worst-case pairTight, roughly equal-sized clustersOne outlier inflates the farthest-pair distance
Average — balances single & completeGeneral-purpose default when unsureSensitive to cluster-size imbalance
Ward — minimises variance increaseBusiness/RFM data, balanced segmentsInherits K-Means' spherical/equal-size assumption
Practical rule: start with Ward (most real segments are round, similarly sized). Get suspicious if the dendrogram forces very unbalanced groups or you suspect non-round structure — then try Single/Average and compare via Silhouette + business sense.

Cutting the dendrogram (choosing K)

Output is a linkage matrix (idx1, idx2, distance, count) — n−1 rows. Cut procedure: find the tallest vertical gap between consecutive merges → cut there → lines crossed = K.

Not guaranteed to find true K — a tall gap can hide two true clusters that merged unusually cheaply. Treat it as a strong first guess; cross-check with Silhouette.
vs. K-Means' elbow: hierarchical's merge-height elbow is read straight off one already-built dendrogram — free, deterministic, no re-fitting or seed sensitivity, unlike re-running K-Means from scratch at every K.

Scale & outliers

  • O(n²) memory, always — the full pairwise distance matrix, unavoidable regardless of implementation speed.
  • Merges are permanent — a bad early merge propagates through the rest of the tree. Fix: cut at K+1/K+2, inspect singleton clusters as outlier candidates, remove, re-cut.
Standard production pattern for large/non-spherical data: sample → run Hierarchical on the sample → read K off its dendrogram → run K-Means/K-Means++ with that K on the full dataset. Fixes K-selection, not shape — for shape at scale, reach for DBSCAN or an approximate method like BIRCH.

algorithm 04 · soft · probabilistic, generative

Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)

Models data as generated from a mixture of K Gaussians. Instead of one hard label, every point gets a responsibility — a probability of belonging to each component, summing to 1. Fit via Expectation-Maximization (EM).

N-D Gaussian PDF $$P(\vec x\mid\vec\mu,\Sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^D|\Sigma|}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(\vec x-\vec\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\vec x-\vec\mu)\right)$$
Probability vs. likelihood — the mix-up interviewers listen for: probability is area under the curve over a range (∈[0,1]); likelihood is curve height at one point (can exceed 1). GMM's E-step uses likelihood, never probability.

Expectation–Maximization

EM alternates two steps: E-step computes every component's responsibility (γ) for every point; M-step recomputes μ, Σ, π as responsibility-weighted statistics.

E-step $$\gamma_{ik}=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j}\pi_j\,\mathcal N(x_i\mid\mu_j,\Sigma_j)} \qquad N_k=\sum_i \gamma_{ik}$$
M-step $$\pi_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{N_k}{n}\qquad \mu_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i}{N_k}\qquad \Sigma_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k^{\text{new}})(x_i-\mu_k^{\text{new}})^T}{N_k}$$

sklearn's .predict() just takes argmax(γ) — a legitimate simplification that discards exactly the info you chose GMM for.

Interview gold: K-Means is a special case of GMM — equal weights (πₖ=1/K), one shared spherical covariance (Σₖ=σ²I), and σ²→0 sharpens soft responsibilities into a hard 0/1 argmax. Under that limit, the M-step's weighted average reduces exactly to K-Means' centroid update.

Choosing K — AIC / BIC

model selection $$AIC = 2p - 2\ln\hat L \qquad BIC = p\ln(n) - 2\ln\hat L$$

BIC penalises complexity harder than AIC as n grows → use BIC for the true number of subgroups (statistically consistent); use AIC for the best predictive fit on new points. Elbow doesn't apply here — it's built for hard-label inertia, not likelihood.

Limitations

  • Elliptical-only shape — baked into the Gaussian formula; no μ/Σ can trace a crescent or ring.
  • More parameters than K-Means → more init-sensitivity (mitigate with n_init) and risk of a near-singular covariance on small/skewed clusters (fix: reg_covar).
  • High-D covariance blows up ($D(D+1)/2$ free entries per component) — run PCA before GMM on high-D data.
K-Means / HierarchicalGMM
MembershipHard — exactly one clusterSoft — probability across all
ShapeSpherical / linkage-dependentElliptical — captures correlation
FittingIterative reassignment / mergingExpectation-Maximization

algorithm 05 · density-based · no K, native noise

DBSCAN

Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise. Every prior algorithm asks "how close is this point to a centre (or ellipse)?" DBSCAN is the first to ask "is there enough company nearby?" — and the first willing to say a point belongs nowhere.

the only two ingredients $$N_\varepsilon(P)=\{q\in D \mid d(P,q)\le\varepsilon\} \quad\text{(P itself counts)} \qquad \text{CORE if } |N_\varepsilon(P)|\ge \text{MinPts}$$
RoleDefinitionCan it grow a cluster?
Core|N_ε(P)| ≥ MinPts — dense on its ownYes — pulls in neighbours
BorderNot core, but inside a core point's ε-neighbourhoodNo — passenger only
NoiseNot core, not reachable from any core pointNever assigned to a cluster
The one asymmetry rule: only core points can pull others in. A border point can be pulled in, but can never do the pulling.
Two boundary gotchas: the core test is ≥, not > (landing exactly on MinPts still counts). A pair of mutually-close points is not automatically a cluster — any group smaller than MinPts is not a valid seed, however tight.

Formal cluster definition

RelationDefinitionWhy it matters
Directly density-reachableq∈N_ε(p) and p is coreAsymmetric — "only hosts can invite"
Density-reachablea chain p=p₁,…,pₙ=q, each step directly reachableExplains how DBSCAN follows curvy/non-round clusters
Density-connected∃ point o such that both p, q are density-reachable from oGlues border points on opposite sides of one cluster together

A cluster is formally a maximal set of density-connected points.

Choosing MinPts and ε

MinPts: absolute floor D+1 (min 3, Ester et al. 1996); practical default 2×D (Sander et al. 1998); the "4" you see everywhere in 2D tutorials is just 2×D at D=2.

ε via k-distance / elbow: for every point, compute distance to its MinPts-th nearest neighbour → sort ascending → plot → the "knee" near the right end = candidate ε.

Use the MinPts-th neighbour, not MinPts−1 (your own distance-to-self = 0 always occupies the free first slot).

Advantages

  • No K, ever — cluster count emerges from density.
  • Genuinely arbitrary shapes — crescents, rings, spirals, via multi-hop reachability.
  • Noise is first-class, not forced — outliers don't distort centroids the way they drag K-Means/GMM.
  • Mostly deterministic — no random initialisation to fix, unlike K-Means/GMM (there are no centroids to seed; the only order-dependence left is which cluster a border point touching two different cores joins first — core and noise labels themselves never change).

Limitations

  • Uniform-density assumption — one global (ε, MinPts) can't serve a dense and a sparse true cluster at once. Not tunable away — structural.
  • Curse of dimensionality — distances concentrate in high-D, both ε and MinPts lose meaning.
  • Single flat partition — one (ε, MinPts) → one clustering, no hierarchy.
HDBSCAN runs DBSCAN across every ε at once and extracts the clusters most stable across it — solves the "one global ε can't serve differently-dense clusters" problem, with ~one parameter (min cluster size). Use plain DBSCAN when density is roughly uniform; reach for HDBSCAN when it genuinely varies.

validity metric · did the clustering actually work?

Silhouette Score

The one internal validity metric from this module — no ground truth needed, works on any algorithm above.

per point, then averaged $$a(p)=\text{avg dist to own cluster} \qquad b(p)=\min_{\text{other }k}\big(\text{avg dist to cluster }k\big) \qquad s(p)=\frac{b(p)-a(p)}{\max(a(p),b(p))}\in[-1,1]$$
Most common mistake: b(p) is not the average distance to all other points — compute the mean distance to each rival cluster separately, then take the minimum (nearest rival). Never shortcut it to distance-to-centroid.
Why not distance-to-centroid? By Jensen's inequality, distance-to-centroid always underestimates the true average member distance — a fixed-direction bias that can flip which cluster counts as "nearest rival," not just add noise.
  • Negative silhouette = unlucky initialisation, or the data doesn't actually support well-separated clusters at that K.
  • Cost: O(n²) — infeasible at scale (1M points ≈ 5×10¹¹ pairwise computations). Sample 5,000–10,000 points for offline K-selection, not live monitoring.
  • Rough quality bar: below ~0.3–0.5 is generally considered thin separation.

interview rapid-fire

Questions You Will Actually Get Asked

Why can't you pick K by minimising WCSS?
WCSS falls monotonically to exactly 0 at K=n. Use the Elbow's diminishing-returns bend, confirmed by Silhouette, then apply business context.
Why does K-Means produce round/convex clusters?
The perpendicular bisector between two centroids is a straight line, so the region closer to one centroid than all others is a convex polygon — a geometric consequence of nearest-centroid assignment, not a design choice.
Does K-Means++ guarantee the optimal clustering?
No. The first centroid is still random, and later picks are weighted, not forced. It's reliably better on average, not a guarantee — pair it with n_init.
Why does K-Means++ square D(x) instead of using it directly?
Squaring widens the gap between near and far points, pushing the weighted draw much harder toward genuinely underserved regions — the same reason WCSS itself squares distance.
Why is b(p) not simply the distance to the rival centroid?
By Jensen's inequality, distance-to-centroid always underestimates the true average member distance — a fixed-direction bias that can flip which rival cluster "wins," not just add noise.
Density-reachable vs. density-connected — what's the difference?
Density-reachable is a directed chain of core-point hops from p to q. Density-connected means both p and q are reachable from some shared point o — needed to unite two border points that can't reach each other directly.
If K-Means is a special case of GMM, is GMM always better?
No. GMM has far more parameters (full covariance per component), so more initialisation sensitivity, more overfitting risk, and possible singular-covariance failures — generality is a trade-off, not a strict upgrade.
Why MinPts and not MinPts−1 in the k-distance plot?
If your neighbour search includes the point itself, the free first slot is always the point's own distance-to-self (0) — so you need the MinPts-th neighbour to get MinPts−1 real others.
What does a negative Silhouette score mean?
Either an unlucky initialisation, or the data genuinely doesn't support well-separated clusters at that K.
Why are early merges in hierarchical clustering risky?
Merges are permanent — once two points/clusters merge, it can never be undone, so a bad early call propagates through the rest of the tree. Practical fix: cut at K+1/K+2, inspect singleton clusters as likely outliers, remove, re-cut.
DBSCAN returns one giant cluster and a lot of noise — what's usually wrong?
ε is too large (or MinPts too low) — genuinely separate groups get "chained" together through sparse in-between points into one blob, while everything else that doesn't reach that chain gets left as noise. Re-check ε via the k-distance elbow.
When would you reach for HDBSCAN over plain DBSCAN?
When true clusters have meaningfully different densities — one global (ε, MinPts) structurally cannot serve both a tight and a sparse cluster at once; HDBSCAN finds each at its own natural density level.

Formula Appendix

K-Means

$d=\sqrt{\sum(p_k-q_k)^2}$
$WCSS=\sum_k\sum_{p\in k}\lVert p-C_k\rVert^2$

K-Means++

$P(x)=\dfrac{D(x)^2}{\sum D(x')^2}$
D(x) = dist to nearest chosen centroid

Hierarchical

Single: $\min d(a,b)$ · Complete: $\max d(a,b)$
Average: $\frac{1}{|A||B|}\sum\sum d(a,b)$
Ward: $\frac{|A||B|}{|A|+|B|}\lVert\mu_A-\mu_B\rVert^2$

GMM

$\gamma_{ik}=\dfrac{\pi_k\mathcal N(x_i|\mu_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal N(x_i|\mu_j,\Sigma_j)}$
$AIC=2p-2\ln\hat L$ · $BIC=p\ln n-2\ln\hat L$

DBSCAN

$N_\varepsilon(P)=\{q\mid d(P,q)\le\varepsilon\}$
Core: $|N_\varepsilon(P)|\ge$ MinPts
MinPts ≥ D+1, practical default 2D

Silhouette Score

$a(p)=$ avg dist, own cluster
$b(p)=\min$ avg dist, nearest rival cluster
$s(p)=\dfrac{b(p)-a(p)}{\max(a(p),b(p))}$
