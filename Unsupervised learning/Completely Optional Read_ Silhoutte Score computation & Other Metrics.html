01 · the tempting shortcut

Why not just use the centroid for b(p)?

In Class 01, computing \(b(p)\) meant: for every other cluster, average the distance from \(p\) to every single member, then take the smallest average. A very reasonable question follows:

"Why not just take the distance from p to each rival cluster's centroid, and use the minimum of those instead? We already have the centroids sitting right there from K-Means — isn't that the same idea, just cheaper?" — the exact shortcut every learner tries once
the proposed shortcut \[ b_{\text{naive}}(p) = \min_{k \,\neq\, \text{own cluster}} \ \lVert p - C_k \rVert \]

One distance per rival cluster's centroid, instead of averaging the distance to every member of that cluster.

It's a fair instinct. It's \(O(K)\) instead of \(O(n)\) per point — dramatically cheaper. If it gave the same answer, we'd teach it that way. It doesn't, and the reason is not a minor rounding difference — it can flip the entire verdict on a point. Here's exactly why.
02 · the mathematical reason

Distance-to-centroid is not the same as average distance-to-members

This isn't a matter of taste — it's a fixed mathematical relationship between the two quantities, true for any cluster, always in the same direction.

Why ‖p − centroid‖ always underestimates the true average distance

In plain English, before the math

A centroid is a single averaged point. Averaging first, then measuring distance, throws away information about how spread out the cluster actually is — it can only make the cluster look more compact from p's point of view than it really is, never less. That's the whole idea; the proof below just makes it precise.

Now the formal version

The Euclidean norm is a convex function. Jensen's inequality says: for any convex function \(f\) and any set of points \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) with mean \(\bar x\),

\[ f(\bar x) \le \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i) \]

Apply this with \(f(x) = \lVert p - x \rVert\) (itself convex in \(x\)) and \(\bar x = C_k\), the centroid of cluster \(k\):

\[ \lVert p - C_k \rVert \;\le\; \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{x_i \in k} \lVert p - x_i \rVert \]
\[\boxed{\;\lVert p - C_k \rVert \ \le\ \tfrac{1}{n_k}\textstyle\sum \lVert p - x_i \rVert\;}\]

In words: distance to the centroid is always ≤ the true average distance to every member.

Equality only holds in the degenerate case where every member of the cluster is equidistant from \(p\) and collinear with it in exactly the right way — essentially never, for real data. In every realistic case, the centroid shortcut systematically under-reports how far \(p\) really is from a rival cluster.

Why this matters for b(p) specifically. \(b(p)\) is supposed to represent "how close is \(p\) to its toughest rival, on average." A systematic underestimate doesn't just add noise — it can make an obviously well-assigned point look artificially close to the wrong cluster, dragging its silhouette score down for no real reason, or worse, flipping which cluster even counts as the "nearest rival" in the first place.
03 · seeing it happen

A counterexample using three ordinary-looking clusters

No rings, no engineered symmetry — just three clusters that could plausibly show up in real customer data, each shaped and placed the way real clusters actually look.

The setup — three clusters, three separate locations

1
Cluster W (p's own cluster) — a small tight group at the origin. \(p=(0,0)\) plus three neighbours: \(w_1=(1,0)\), \(w_2=(0,1)\), \(w_3=(-1,0)\).
2
Cluster Z (a rival) — an ordinary tight cluster of 3 customers sitting off to the right, near \((7.5, 0)\). Nothing unusual about it.
3
Cluster Y (a rival) — 5 customers spread along a diagonal, from \((1,9)\) down to \((9,1)\). This is a completely normal cluster shape — think "a spectrum of customers ranging from high-frequency/low-spend to low-frequency/high-spend," all grouped together because they share some other trait.
4
The trap: Y isn't tight — it's stretched out. Its centroid lands in the empty middle of the diagonal, at (5,5) — a point where no actual customer sits — which happens to be noticeably closer to p than the diagonal's real endpoints are.
a(p) and b(p) never touch the same cluster. \(a(p)\) only looks at p's own cluster, W. \(b(p)\) only looks at the two rivals, Y and Z. W plays no role in the b(p) calculation below.

Notice what actually went wrong. Z's members really are, on average, closer to p (7.50) than Y's members are (8.08) — Z should win. But because Y is spread out and Z is tight, the naive shortcut compares Y's artificially shrunk centroid distance (7.07) against Z's honest one (7.50) — and picks the wrong cluster. This is exactly the kind of comparison you make every time you compute Silhouette Score: comparing one cluster's honesty against another's is only fair if you measure both the same way — by their real members, not a single averaged stand-in point.
04 · resolving the trade-off

So is there any legitimate shortcut? Yes — sampling.

Class 01's efficiency discussion (Section 20) already gave the honest answer, and it's worth contrasting directly with the centroid shortcut here.

✗ Centroid shortcut

  • Replaces \(n_k\) real distances with 1 fake one
  • Mathematically biased in a fixed direction (Jensen's inequality) — not random noise, a systematic error
  • Can flip which cluster counts as "nearest rival"
  • Cheap, but the number it produces isn't the quantity you asked for
vs

✓ Random sampling

  • Still averages over real member distances — just fewer of them
  • Unbiased — a random sample's mean is an unbiased estimator of the true mean, by definition
  • Never changes which cluster is nearest, only adds a little variance to the estimate
  • Tunable — more samples, better estimate, still far cheaper than the full \(O(n^2)\)
The real lesson. "Faster" and "wrong" are independent axes. Sampling is faster and correct in expectation. The centroid shortcut is faster and systematically wrong. Always ask which one you're trading before accepting a shortcut.

06 · new metric

Davies-Bouldin Index

For every cluster, find its worst rival — the one it's most easily confused with — then average that worst-case confusion across all clusters. Lower means clusters are tight and well separated.

Davies-Bouldin — full definition \[ S_i = \frac{1}{n_i}\sum_{p \in i} \lVert p - C_i \rVert \qquad M_{ij} = \lVert C_i - C_j \rVert \] \[ R_{ij} = \frac{S_i + S_j}{M_{ij}} \qquad D_i = \max_{j \neq i} R_{ij} \] \[ \boxed{\;DB = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^K D_i\;} \]

\(S_i\) — average spread of cluster \(i\) around its own centroid. \(M_{ij}\) — centroid-to-centroid separation.

Reading \(R_{ij}\). It's a ratio of "how spread out are these two clusters combined" to "how far apart are their centres." Big spread + small separation → big \(R_{ij}\) → these two clusters are easily confused. Each cluster's \(D_i\) keeps only its single worst confusion.

This is exactly the kind of interaction DB captures that a pure distance metric wouldn't.

07 · new metric

Calinski-Harabasz Index (Variance Ratio Criterion)

Borrowed directly from ANOVA's F-statistic: how much variance sits between clusters compared to within them, normalised by degrees of freedom. Higher means the clusters explain more of the data's total spread than chance would.

Calinski-Harabasz — full definition \[ BSS = \sum_{i=1}^K n_i \lVert C_i - \bar{X} \rVert^2 \qquad WSS = \sum_{i=1}^K \sum_{p \in i} \lVert p - C_i \rVert^2 \] \[ \boxed{\;CH = \frac{BSS/(K-1)}{WSS/(n-K)}\;} \]

BSS — between-cluster sum of squares. WSS — within-cluster sum of squares, this is WCSS from Class 01.

It's literally the WCSS you already know, plus a new "between" counterpart, ratio'd together the way an F-test compares explained vs. unexplained variance.

Step 1 — the overall centroid (mean of all 18 points)

\[ \bar{X} = (5.083,\ 4.250) \]

Step 2 — between-cluster sum of squares, BSS

Cluster\(n_i\)\(C_i\)\(\lVert C_i - \bar X \rVert^2\)\(n_i \cdot \lVert C_i-\bar X\rVert^2\)
15(1.8, 1.8)16.7883.91
25(8.2, 2.8)11.8259.08
38(5.19, 6.69)5.9547.62
\[ BSS = 83.91 + 59.08 + 47.62 = \mathbf{190.61} \]

Step 3 — within-cluster sum of squares, WSS (= WCSS, already computed in Class 01)

\[ WSS = 5.60 + 5.60 + 25.94 = \mathbf{37.14} \]

Step 4 — combine

\[ CH = \frac{190.61/(3-1)}{37.14/(18-3)} = \frac{95.31}{2.476} = \mathbf{38.49} \]
Reading 38.49. There's no fixed "good" threshold — CH is a relative, comparative score, most useful for ranking different values of K against each other on the same dataset (Section 9 does exactly this). On its own, a large positive number simply means between-cluster variance dominates within-cluster variance by a wide margin — a healthy sign.
from sklearn.metrics import calinski_harabasz_score calinski_harabasz_score(X, labels) # -> 38.49 — matches exactly
08 · new metric

Dunn Index

The most literal translation of "tight clusters, far apart" into one ratio: the single closest pair of points across any two clusters, divided by the single widest pair of points inside any one cluster.

Dunn Index — full definition \[ \delta_{\min} = \min_{i \neq j}\ \min_{p \in i,\ q \in j} \lVert p - q \rVert \qquad \Delta_{\max} = \max_{i}\ \max_{p,q \in i} \lVert p - q \rVert \] \[ \boxed{\;Dunn = \dfrac{\delta_{\min}}{\Delta_{\max}}\;} \]

\(\delta_{\min}\) — closest distance between any two points in different clusters. \(\Delta_{\max}\) — widest distance between any two points in the same cluster (the cluster's "diameter").

Step 1 — find the single closest inter-cluster pair, across all 18 points

Checking every cross-cluster pair (not just centroids — every actual point), the closest pair turns out to be I (in Cluster 2) and Q (in Cluster 3):

\[ \delta_{\min} = d(I, Q) = \sqrt{(8-6.5)^2+(4-5.5)^2} = \mathbf{2.121} \]

Step 2 — find the single widest intra-cluster pair

Checking every same-cluster pair, the widest gap is inside Cluster 3, between N and R — our largest, most spread-out cluster:

\[ \Delta_{\max} = d(N, R) = \sqrt{(5-4)^2+(9-4.5)^2} = \mathbf{4.610} \]
Dunn's entire verdict rests on exactly two points out of eighteen — the single closest cross-cluster pair (I, Q) and single farthest same-cluster pair (N, R).

Step 3 — combine

\[ Dunn = \frac{2.121}{4.610} = \mathbf{0.460} \]
Notice the fragility already. Two other clusters, tight and well-separated, contribute nothing to this score — the entire index is decided by whichever single pair happens to be closest, and whichever single pair happens to be farthest. One noisy customer in an otherwise perfect clustering can single-handedly tank the Dunn Index. We'll see this bite in Section 9.

They don't all agree. Silhouette and Davies-Bouldin both peak/bottom at K=3 — matching the known true structure. Calinski-Harabasz and Dunn both peak at K=4 instead. Same data, same clusterings, four different "best K" opinions collapsing into two camps. Worth understanding exactly why.

Why does K=4 tempt Calinski-Harabasz and Dunn?

Running K-Means with K=4 on this data doesn't invent a new region — it takes Cluster 3 (our loosest cluster, boundary customers P, Q, R included) and splits it into two: \(\{K,L,M,N,O\}\) and \(\{P,Q,R\}\).

K=4 in practice: Clusters 1 and 2 stay untouched. Cluster 3 alone splits into a tighter top group and the boundary trio P, Q, R.

Calinski-Harabasz's blind spot

CH rewards any reduction in within-cluster variance, weighted by degrees of freedom — it has no concept of "is this split meaningful." Splitting the one loose cluster (\(S_3{=}1.665\), well above the other two clusters' \(1.043\)) mechanically shrinks WSS a lot, so CH rewards it — even though visually, P/Q/R were always boundary customers, not a genuine fourth segment.

Dunn's blind spot

Recall Dunn's \(\Delta_{\max}\) at K=3 was driven entirely by one pair: N↔R (4.61). Splitting Cluster 3 removes that specific pair from being intra-cluster at all — N and R now live in different clusters, so they no longer count against \(\Delta_{\max}\). Dunn's score jumps for a reason that has nothing to do with overall clustering quality — it's an artefact of exactly one pair of points changing cluster.

Meanwhile, Silhouette and Davies-Bouldin don't get fooled — both average across many points/clusters rather than hinging on a single extreme pair or a single loose cluster's variance, so a marginal, boundary-driven split at K=4 doesn't create a false improvement large enough to beat the genuinely well-separated K=3 structure.
10 · the final ranking

Best, worst, and why — for this class's kind of data

RankMetricVerdict
1 — most reliableDavies-BouldinGot K=3 right, cheap to compute (\(O(n \cdot K)\), no pairwise sampling needed), and its cluster-vs-worst-rival structure is intuitive to explain to stakeholders. Best all-round default alongside Silhouette.
2Silhouette ScoreAlso got K=3 right and gives the richest diagnostic — a score per point, not just per clustering, which is invaluable for finding exactly which customers are ambiguous (Class 01, P/Q/R). Costs \(O(n^2)\); sample for large data.
3Calinski-HarabaszExtremely fast (one pass, closed form, no pairwise distances at all) and useful as a quick first filter across many K values — but shown here to have a real bias toward over-splitting the least uniform cluster. Use as a fast screen, not the final word.
4 — least reliableDunn IndexTheoretically the most elegant one-sentence definition ("closest gap over widest spread") — and the most fragile in practice. A single outlier or single boundary pair can dominate the entire score in either direction. Treat as a sanity check, never as the deciding vote.
The general principle, not just for this dataset. Metrics built from averages (Silhouette's per-point average, Davies-Bouldin's per-cluster average spread) are naturally more robust to a handful of odd points. Metrics built from a single extremum — Dunn's closest/farthest pair — or a single aggregate ratio sensitive to uneven cluster density — Calinski-Harabasz — can be swayed disproportionately by exactly the kind of messy boundary cases real customer data always has. When metrics disagree, trust the ones averaging over more evidence.
