01 · nutshell revision

Class 01, in three sentences

K-Means, in a nutshell

Pick K starting points → assign every customer to its nearest centroid → move each centroid to the mean of its assigned customers → repeat until nothing changes. That's the entire algorithm.

Elbow Curve, in a nutshell

WCSS (total squared distance to centroid) always falls as K increases. We look for the point where it stops falling sharply and starts flattening — the "elbow" — as a candidate K.

Silhouette Score, in a nutshell

For each point, compare its average distance to its own cluster (a) against its average distance to the nearest other cluster (b): $s=\frac{b-a}{\max(a,b)}$. Closer to +1 is better.

Today builds directly on top of these three ideas — applied to a real dataset, checked with real scrutiny, and pushed until we hit K-Means's actual limits.
02 · the real dataset

The Online Retail problem statement

Everything today is grounded in one real dataset — a UK-based online retailer's transaction log — not synthetic points.

"We have a raw transaction log — every line item of every order, for every customer, over roughly a year. We want to segment customers into meaningful groups so marketing can treat them differently. There is no 'customer type' column anywhere." — the actual business problem behind this notebook

What the raw data actually looks like

ColumnWhat it is
InvoiceNoOrder/invoice identifier — one invoice can span multiple rows (one per product)
StockCodeProduct identifier
QuantityUnits of that product bought in this line item
InvoiceDateTimestamp of the transaction
UnitPricePrice per unit for that product
CustomerIDWhich customer made this purchase — missing for a meaningful chunk of rows
The key observation: this is a transaction-level log, not a customer-level table. There is no row that says "Customer 17850 is a VIP." To even begin clustering customers, we first have to build a one-row-per-customer table ourselves — that construction step is RFM, and it's where most of today's real thinking happens, not in the K-Means call itself.

What we want, precisely: convert this raw log into exactly one row per customer, capturing enough of their purchase behaviour that "similar rows" genuinely means "similar customers" — then let K-Means group those rows.

03 · building the features, with reasons

Creating RFM — step by step, why before what

Three numbers, each answering a different plain-English question about a customer. Every step below explains why it exists before showing the code.

Step 1 — Load and clean

Why first: we need to know what a "row" is before engineering anything. Each row here is one product line-item on one invoice — not yet a customer.

retail = pd.read_csv("Online_Retail.csv", sep=',', encoding="ISO-8859-1", header=0) retail['InvoiceDate'] = pd.to_datetime(retail['InvoiceDate'], format="%d-%m-%Y %H:%M") order_wise = retail.dropna() # rows with no CustomerID are unusable for segmentation

Why drop, not impute: a missing CustomerID isn't a value we can guess — the row simply cannot be attributed to any customer, so it can't contribute to that customer's profile.

Step 2 — Monetary (M): how much does this customer spend?

Why this exact formula: a line-item's value is quantity × unit price — nothing else in the raw columns gives us ₹ value directly.

order_wise['Amount'] = order_wise.Quantity * order_wise.UnitPrice monetary = order_wise.groupby("CustomerID").Amount.sum().reset_index()

Why sum, not average: total lifetime spend is what tells us a customer's overall value to the business — an average would treat a customer with 1 huge order the same as a customer with 50 small orders totalling the same amount, which are very different business relationships.

Step 3 — Frequency (F): how often does this customer transact?

frequency = order_wise.groupby("CustomerID").InvoiceNo.count().reset_index() frequency.columns = ["CustomerID", "Frequency"]

Why count matters on its own, separate from Monetary: two customers can have identical total spend, but one visits weekly in small amounts while the other visits twice a year in bulk — completely different engagement, invisible if we only looked at Monetary.

Step 4 — Recency (R): how recently did this customer last buy?

maximum = max(order_wise.InvoiceDate) + pd.DateOffset(days=1) recency = order_wise[['CustomerID','InvoiceDate']] recency['diff'] = maximum - recency.InvoiceDate df = recency.groupby('CustomerID')['diff'].min().reset_index() df.columns = ["CustomerID", "Recency"]

Why "max date + 1 day" as the reference point: we need one fixed "today" to measure "days ago" from. Using the dataset's own latest transaction (plus a day, so even the most recent buyer has a non-zero recency) avoids hard-coding an arbitrary external date — the reference point is derived entirely from the data itself.

Why min of the difference, not max or mean: we want each customer's most recent purchase — the smallest gap to "today" — not their oldest purchase or an average across their whole history.

Step 5 — Merge into one RFM table

RFM = frequency.merge(monetary, on="CustomerID").merge(df, on="CustomerID")

What this produces: the first true "one row per customer" table — exactly what was missing from the raw log.

Step 6 — Outlier treatment (IQR, on all three columns)

for col in ['Amount', 'Frequency', 'Recency']: Q1, Q3 = RFM[col].quantile(0.25), RFM[col].quantile(0.75) IQR = Q3 - Q1 RFM = RFM[(RFM[col] >= Q1 - 1.5*IQR) & (RFM[col] <= Q3 + 1.5*IQR)]

Why here, specifically: K-Means moves centroids to the mean, and the mean is dragged hard by extreme values (Class 01's outlier sensitivity, showing up for real). One bulk wholesale-style order can single-handedly distort a centroid — this step removes exactly that risk before it ever reaches the algorithm.

Step 7 — Scale

standard_scaler = StandardScaler() RFM_norm = standard_scaler.fit_transform(RFM[['Frequency','Amount','Recency']])

Why: Amount lives in the hundreds/thousands, Frequency in single/double digits, Recency in days — wildly different numeric ranges that would let Amount silently dominate every Euclidean distance calculation without this step.

Where we land: three scaled numbers per customer — Frequency, Amount, Recency — each with a clear business meaning, each engineered for a specific reason. This RFM_norm table is what K-Means actually sees from here on.

05 · the three cases you'll actually run into

Elbow vs Silhouette — three scenarios, worked through properly

Scenario 1 — both are good (this dataset's actual outcome)

Elbow bends cleanly at K=3. Silhouette independently peaks at K=3. Two different formulas, agreeing.

Decision: K=3, with confidence. This is the strongest signal available without running a live experiment — proceed to cluster profiling.

Scenario 2 — elbow is good, silhouette is poor

Suppose the elbow still bends cleanly at K=3 — but at K=3, Silhouette only comes out to 0.28 (weak; below roughly 0.3–0.5 is generally considered thin separation).

What this combination actually means: WCSS tells you clusters are getting tighter as K increases — that's a fact about compactness alone. A poor Silhouette at the same K tells you the clusters, despite being individually tight, are sitting close to each other — poor separation. You can have compact clusters that still overlap badly.

What to actually do:

1
Don't just trust the elbow and ship K=3 anyway — a low Silhouette is telling you something the elbow structurally cannot see (elbow only ever measures intra-cluster tightness, never inter-cluster separation).
2
Check feature scaling first — an unscaled dominant feature is one of the most common causes of poor separation despite a clean elbow.
3
Check if the features themselves actually separate customers on anything meaningful — sometimes the true structure just isn't there at 3 groups, and a different K or different features are needed.

Scenario 3 — both look good... but is that actually good enough?

Elbow bends at K=3. Silhouette is a healthy 0.55 at K=3. By every geometric measure, this is a strong result.

The question neither metric can answer: "good enough for what?" A Silhouette of 0.55 confirms the 3 groups are real and separated — it says nothing about whether those 3 groups differ on retention, respond differently to a campaign, or justify the cost of building 3 separate marketing treatments. "Geometrically good" and "good enough to act on" are different bars, and only one of them is measured by Silhouette.

This is the exact gap this class exists to close — the next section gives you the actual checklist for answering "good enough for what?" properly.

06 · closing the gap

Priority order — how to evaluate a cluster once it's formed

A fixed sequence, cheapest check first, so you never skip straight to "ship it" on Silhouette alone.

1
Geometric check — WCSS + Silhouette. Cheap, fast, catches obviously broken clusterings (one giant blob, empty clusters) before spending any more time.
2
Profile the clusters — look at actual mean Recency/Frequency/Monetary per cluster. Do the numbers tell a coherent story a human can name ("frequent big spenders" vs "one-time small buyers")?
3
Stability check — rerun with a different random seed, or on a random 80% sample. Do you get roughly the same 3 groups back, or do they shuffle unpredictably?
4
Business validation — the only step with a real answer to "good enough for what?" Does treating these 3 groups differently (different campaigns, different retention offers) actually move a real number — conversion, retention, revenue?

What "bad" looks like, and what to do about it

What you observeLikely causeWhat to try
Clusters don't separate on any RFM dimensionWeak signal in the chosen featuresAdd more behavioural features (category preference, discount usage), or reconsider the entity/window
One cluster is huge, others tinyAn unscaled dominant feature, or genuine imbalance in customer baseRe-check StandardScaler was applied correctly; if imbalance is real, it may be a legitimate finding, not a bug
Cluster profiles don't make business senseOutliers not fully treated, or K chosen without enough evidenceRevisit the IQR step; re-run Elbow/Silhouette across a wider K range

08 · a different kind of problem

Random initialization — the issue that has nothing to do with features

Everything so far assumed K-Means starts sensibly. It doesn't, by default — the starting centroids are picked uniformly at random from the data, and that single choice can swing the entire result.

WHAT YOU SEE: same data, same K=4, only the random seed changed across 5 runs. NOTICE: WCSS ranges from 362 (great) to 8,256 (badly stuck) purely from luck of the draw. WHY IT MATTERS: a scheduled retrain with no seed control can hand a customer a completely different segment next month, for no real behavioural reason at all.
The fix is NOT a new algorithm. K-Means++ changes exactly one thing: how the starting centroids are chosen. Every Assignment step, every Update step, every convergence check afterward is identical, unmodified K-Means.

K-Means++'s rule, in plain English

1
Pick the first centroid uniformly at random from the real data points — identical to plain random init.
2
For every remaining point, compute its distance to the nearest already-chosen centroid.
3
Square that distance, turn all the squared distances into probabilities, and draw the next centroid using those probabilities — far-away points are much more likely to be picked, but it's a genuine weighted random draw, not a forced pick.
4
Repeat steps 2–3 until all K centroids are chosen.
WHAT YOU SEE: same data, same K, same seed — only the init rule differs. NOTICE: random init's centroids (left) can clump in one region; K-Means++'s (right) spread out on their own, with zero forcing.
WHAT YOU SEE: 20 independent runs each. NOTICE: random init's WCSS spans 362 to over 13,000; K-Means++ lands on WCSS=362 — the best arrangement — in every single run.

Does K-Means++ always guarantee a better clustering?

No — and this is worth being precise about. K-Means++ is always at least probabilistically better than plain random init, never a guarantee of the single best possible clustering. Two reasons why:
  • The first centroid is still pure random chance. Every centroid after the first is weighted toward far-away points, but that very first pick has no such guidance — an unlucky first draw can still nudge the whole chain of choices toward a worse arrangement.
  • It's a weighted draw, not a forced pick. As Section 09/10 show explicitly, a low-probability point genuinely can still get chosen (that's what "probability", not "certainty", means) — so the same dataset can still produce different initial centroids across different runs, just with a much narrower, much better spread of outcomes than plain random init (Section 08's chart proves this numerically: 362 vs. up to 13,000+).
The accurate claim to make: "K-Means++ is consistently better on average, and far more reliable run-to-run, than random init — not a guarantee of the global optimum on every single run." This is exactly why production pipelines still combine K-Means++ with multiple restarts (n_init) — the smarter initialization reduces bad-luck risk, it doesn't eliminate randomness altogether.

import numpy as np def kmeans_pp_next_centroid(X, chosen_idx, rng): chosen = X[chosen_idx] d = np.array([min(np.linalg.norm(p - c) for c in chosen) for p in X]) d[chosen_idx] = 0 probs = (d ** 2) / np.sum(d ** 2) cumulative = np.cumsum(probs) # matches the "cumulative probability" column above r = rng.random() # uniform random draw in [0,1) next_idx = np.searchsorted(cumulative, r) # finds which "slice" r landed in return next_idx

Why the MINIMUM distance, and not the maximum or the average?

WHAT YOU SEE: P4 is fairly close to C1 (7.81) but far from C2 (8.60). NOTICE: we use 7.81 — the smaller one. WHY: P4 is already reasonably well-served by C1 — an existing centroid is nearby, so P4 doesn't need a new centroid dedicated to it. Its true "gap" — how underserved it is by the current centroid set — is measured by its closest existing centroid, not its farthest. Using the maximum here would wrongly treat P4 as high-priority just because it happens to be far from one specific centroid, ignoring that another centroid already covers it well.
The one-line rule: "distance to nearest existing centroid" measures how well-covered a point already is. Taking the MIN is what makes K-Means++ target genuinely under-served regions of the data — not just any region that's far from any one arbitrary centroid.

Limitations of K-Means / K-Means++ — shape, size, density

K-Means++ fixes where you start. It changes nothing about what a centroid can represent. These three limitations exist regardless of initialization — proven with real code, not just claimed.

from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs, make_circles, make_moons from sklearn.cluster import KMeans blobs_X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=300, centers=4, cluster_std=0.8, random_state=42) circles_X, _ = make_circles(n_samples=300, factor=0.5, noise=0.05, random_state=42) moons_X, _ = make_moons(n_samples=300, noise=0.07, random_state=42) uneven_X, _ = make_blobs(n_samples=300, centers=[[0,0],[4,4],[8,0]], cluster_std=[0.5,1.5,0.3], random_state=42)
WHAT YOU SEE: top row, raw data. Bottom row, K-Means's actual output (colour = cluster, ✕ = centroid). WHAT K-MEANS THINKS: "place K centroids, assign every point to its nearest one." WHAT WE EXPECTED: rings and crescents separated as rings and crescents. WHY THEY DIFFER: nearest-centroid boundaries are always round/convex — they structurally cannot follow a ring or a curve. LESSON: Blobs succeed because the true structure matches K-Means's own geometry; the other three fail because it doesn't.

Why specifically a CIRCULAR (round) boundary — and not, say, a square or triangle?

This is worth making completely explicit. K-Means assigns a point to whichever centroid is nearest, using Euclidean distance. The set of all points equally close to two centroids is always a straight line (the perpendicular bisector between them) — and the set of points closer to one specific centroid than to any other centroid is the region bounded by straight lines on every side: a convex polygon. With enough far-apart centroids around a single one, that polygon approaches a circle. It is never a square, triangle, or crescent by the algorithm's own construction — the shape isn't a design choice, it's a direct mathematical consequence of "assign to nearest centroid, under straight-line distance."

The test that proves it: replace Euclidean distance with a different metric, and the "natural" boundary shape changes too (e.g. Manhattan distance produces diamond-shaped boundaries instead of round ones) — confirming the round shape comes from the distance metric, not from anything about K-Means's high-level logic.

Problem 1 — Shape (non-globular clusters)

Rings and crescents are the textbook failure — a single centroid, by construction, can only "own" a round patch of space. A ring-shaped true cluster has points on all sides of where its centroid would sit — K-Means has no way to draw a boundary that curves around empty space in the middle.

Problem 2 — Size (clusters of very different sizes)

K-Means implicitly assumes every cluster claims roughly the same amount of "territory." When one true cluster is much larger than another, points near the boundary get assigned by raw nearest-centroid distance — which can unfairly favour the smaller cluster's centroid (since it's tightly packed nearby) over the true, correct membership in the larger, more spread-out cluster.

Problem 3 — Density (clusters of very different density)

The Unequal Density panel above shows this directly — a small, tight, high-density cluster and a large, loose, low-density cluster in the same dataset. K-Means's nearest-centroid rule doesn't know one cluster is "supposed to be" tighter than the other; it just measures distance, so loose-cluster points near the boundary can get pulled into the tight cluster's territory.

WHAT YOU SEE: K-Means++, 10 restarts, best possible initialization, on the moon-shaped data. NOTICE: still a straight-line split. THE POINT THIS PROVES: initialization and shape are two completely independent problems — Section 08–10 fixed the first; nothing fixes the second except switching away from a centroid-based algorithm entirely.
The one sentence to close on: none of these three limitations mean K-Means is broken — every failure above is exactly what "assign to nearest centroid, minimise squared distance" is mathematically supposed to produce. The skill isn't avoiding K-Means; it's recognising, from the data itself, when its assumptions are being stretched past where they hold.
